\begin{abstract}
We study braided-symmetric derivations of an algebra over a field.
These are maps that satisfy the Leibniz rule for the product symmetrized by an involution of $A \otimes A$.
The identity involution gives the ordinary derivations when $2$ is invertible.
The swap gives the Jordan derivations.
The negative of the identity gives all linear endomorphisms, and the negative of the swap gives the Lie derivations.
We then twist the swap by an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$.
The Leibniz defect of a map that commutes with $\alpha$ decides when it is a twisted derivation.
For a commutative reduced algebra with $2$ invertible, the twisted maps are exactly the derivations that commute with $\alpha$.

On semiprime algebras of characteristic different from $2$ the Jordan maps among them are exactly those derivations.
On the algebra $\mathbb{F}_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^3)$ the twisted derivations (those commuting with $\alpha$) and the $\alpha$-derivations do not contain each other.

Nilpotent elements show that reducedness is needed. Characteristic $2$ shows that the invertibility of $2$ is needed. A four-dimensional example shows that reducedness and commutativity cannot both be dropped.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Throughout, $K$ is a field and an algebra means a unital associative $K$-algebra.
We take all maps between algebras to be $K$-linear.
We say so when a result cited below holds more generally for additive maps on rings.

A \emph{derivation} of an algebra $A$ is a linear map $D \colon A \to A$ with $D(xy) = D(x)y + xD(y)$ for all $x, y \in A$.
The notion was put on an abstract footing by \citet[Part I]{Jacobson1937}, who studied the derivations of an arbitrary nonassociative algebra.
\citet[\S\S2 and 4]{Hochschild1945} defined the cocycles of an associative algebra, and its $1$-cocycles are the derivations.

Two further notions appear in the dictionary of this paper. % claim-ok: factual claim about this paper
For $x, y \in A$ we write $[x,y] = xy - yx$ for the commutator and $x \circ y = xy + yx$ for the Jordan product.
A \emph{Lie derivation} is a linear map with $D([x,y]) = [D(x), y] + [x, D(y)]$ \citep[p.~183]{Martindale1964}.
A \emph{Jordan derivation} of $A$ is a linear map with $D(x \circ y) = D(x) \circ y + x \circ D(y)$ for all $x, y \in A$.

The sum $xy + yx$ is twice the Jordan product $\tfrac12 (xy + yx)$ of an associative algebra \citep[Example 2.1]{BresarZelmanov2025}.
Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner form a product $ab$ from the two products $a \times b$ and $b \times a$ of an associative system, and call this quasi-multiplication \citep[p.~64]{JordanVonNeumannWigner1934}.

\citet[p.~1104]{Herstein1957} takes the sum $ab + ba$ without the half.
We follow Herstein and do not divide by $2$, so that characteristic $2$ is allowed.

The three notions are the cases $f = x_1x_2$, $x_1 \circ x_2$ and $[x_1, x_2]$ of an $f$-derivation: \citet[\S1]{Benkovic2015} treats derivations, Jordan derivations and Lie derivations as $f$-derivations, so that one identity for a multilinear polynomial $f$ covers all three.
\citet[Theorem 4.3]{BeidarBresarChebotarMartindale2001} studied maps that act as derivations on a fixed multilinear polynomial, earlier than Benkovi\v{c}.

The results below need three notions for a ring $R$. % claim-ok: factual claim about the paper
We call $R$ \emph{prime} if $aRb = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$ \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
We call $R$ \emph{semiprime} if $aRa = 0$ implies $a = 0$ \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
We call $R$ \emph{$2$-torsion-free} if $2a = 0$ implies $a = 0$ \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
So every prime ring is semiprime.
Herstein proved that every Jordan derivation of a prime ring of characteristic different from $2$ is a derivation \citep[Theorem 3.1]{Herstein1957}.
Every derivation is a Jordan derivation, and the converse is false in general \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
\citet[Corollary 5]{Cusack1975} extended this result of Herstein to $2$-torsion-free semiprime rings, and \citet[Theorem 1]{Bresar1988} gave an independent proof.

Let $A$ be an algebra over $K$ with product $\mu$.
Let $\sigma \colon A \otimes A \to A \otimes A$ be a $K$-linear map with $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{id}$, that is, an involution of $A \otimes A$; the swap $\tau(x \otimes y) = y \otimes x$ is one.
It gives a product of the shape
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma-product}
x \cdot_\sigma y = \mu(x \otimes y) + \mu(\sigma(x \otimes y)).
\end{equation}
An associative algebra becomes a Jordan algebra when its product $xy$ is replaced by $x \circ y = \tfrac12(xy + yx)$, and this symmetrized product has the shape above \citep[Example 2.1]{BresarZelmanov2025}.

For $\sigma = \tau$ this is twice the Jordan product $x \circ y$.
Put $\mu_\sigma = \mu + \mu \circ \sigma$.
Since $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{id}$, we get $\mu_\sigma \circ \sigma = \mu_\sigma$, that is, the following triangle commutes.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\sigma"] \arrow[rd, "\mu_\sigma"'] & A \otimes A \arrow[d, "\mu_\sigma"] \\
& A
\end{tikzcd}
\]
Example~\ref{ex:not-associative} shows that a twisted version of this product can fail to be associative and to have a unit.

A linear map $D \colon A \to A$ is a \emph{braided-symmetric derivation} for $\sigma$ when % ours: the term and the formalization are ours (ledger: own definition)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma-leibniz}
D(x \cdot_\sigma y) = D(x) \cdot_\sigma y + x \cdot_\sigma D(y).
\end{equation}
So \eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz} is the Leibniz rule for the symmetrized product.
The set of these maps is a $K$-subspace of $\mathrm{End}_K(A)$, since the condition is linear in $D$.
We write $\BSDer_\sigma(A)$ for it.
Other authors have studied \emph{braided derivations}, which use a braiding operator directly \citep[\S2]{Rozanski1996} \citep[\S4.1, Definition 14]{Huru2006}. % ours: the comparison with their notion is ours (ledger: own comparison)
Remark~\ref{rem:distinction-from-braided} keeps the two notions apart.
Our identity uses only $\mu$, $\sigma$ and $D$.
We use the word \emph{braided} by analogy with these braiding operators: no braided monoidal category is involved, and $\sigma$ is any $K$-linear involution of $A \otimes A$. % ours: the explanation of our terminological choice is ours

We need two words on maps of $A$ before the next step.
An \emph{automorphism} of $A$ is a bijective $K$-linear map $\alpha \colon A \to A$ with $\alpha(xy) = \alpha(x)\alpha(y)$ and $\alpha(1) = 1$.
An \emph{anti-automorphism} satisfies $\alpha(xy) = \alpha(y)\alpha(x)$ and $\alpha(1) = 1$ instead. % ours: standard algebraic definitions stated inline
In both cases $\alpha$ is \emph{involutive} if $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$. % ours: standard algebraic definition stated inline

We now describe the main results. % claim-ok: factual description of the results of this paper
Our first step is a dictionary.
Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification} shows that the identity gives exactly the derivations when $2$ is invertible, and the swap gives exactly the Jordan derivations. The two sign changes give all linear endomorphisms and the Lie derivations.

Our second step twists the swap by an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$.
For an involutive $\alpha$ we put $\sigma_\alpha(x \otimes y) = \alpha(y) \otimes \alpha(x)$, and we write $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ for the maps of $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ that commute with $\alpha$; we call them \emph{twisted derivations}.
The twisted ($\sigma$-)derivations of the literature are a different notion: an \emph{$\alpha$-derivation} satisfies $D(xy) = D(x)\alpha(y) + \alpha(x)D(y)$ \citep[\S1]{HosseiniKarizaki2019}.

The next definition names the defect that measures the failure of the Leibniz rule. % claim-ok: factual description of the definition that follows
\begin{definition}\label{def:defect} % ours: the name is ours; the map is classical (Cusack 1975)
The \emph{Leibniz defect} of a linear map $D \colon A \to A$ is the bilinear map $E_D \colon A \times A \to A$ given, for $x, y \in A$, by
\[
E_D(x,y) = D(xy) - D(x)y - xD(y).
\]
\end{definition}
The name is ours, and the map is classical: \citet[\S2]{Cusack1975} writes it $d(a,b) = D(ab) - aDb - (Da)b$ and studies Jordan derivations through it.

Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} describes the twisted derivations through this defect.

Corollary~\ref{cor:twisted-semiprime} shows that, on $2$-torsion-free semiprime algebras, the Jordan maps among the twisted derivations are the derivations commuting with $\alpha$.

An element $x$ of an algebra is \emph{nilpotent} if $x^n = 0$ for some $n \geq 1$, and the algebra is \emph{reduced} if its only nilpotent element is zero. % ours: standard algebraic definitions stated inline, cited at first use in Section 2
Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} treats commutative reduced algebras and needs no Jordan hypothesis.
Example~\ref{ex:twisted-nilpotent} shows that the reduced hypothesis is needed, and Example~\ref{ex:twisted-char-two} shows the same for the invertibility of $2$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:twisted} shows that the twisted derivations and the $\alpha$-derivations on one algebra do not contain each other.

The dictionary itself is classical. % claim-ok: the four products are the classical products of this section
The four structure-free involutions, those that use nothing of $A$ beyond its $K$-vector space, give the products $2xy$, $xy + yx$, $0$ and $xy - yx$.
Three of these appear in the notation $f(x,y) = \beta xy + \gamma yx$ of \citet[Remark 1.4]{Benkovic2015}.
That remark assigns a derivation to $\gamma \ne \pm\beta$, a Jordan derivation to $\gamma = \beta$ and a Lie derivation to $\gamma = -\beta$, under the regularity hypotheses stated there.
The degenerate zero product $\beta = \gamma = 0$ lies outside its cases.

The contribution of this paper has four parts, stated below. % claim-ok: factual description of the structure of this paper
Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} turns each of the problems below into a linear condition.
Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} needs no Jordan hypothesis.
Proposition~\ref{prop:twisted} shows that neither of the two classes contains the other.
Remark~\ref{rem:commutativity-needed} gives a counterexample when commutativity and reducedness are dropped together, and reduces the remaining case.

We follow this order.
Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations} recalls the classical notions, defines braided-symmetric derivations and treats the four structure-free involutions, those that use nothing of $A$ beyond its $K$-vector space. They are the only such involutions when $2$ is invertible, and Remark~\ref{rem:natural-involutions} records the others in characteristic $2$. The four collapse to two in characteristic $2$ (and, in that case, to one when $\dim_K A \le 1$).
Section~\ref{sec:twisted} treats the twisted swap through the Leibniz defect.
Section~\ref{sec:twisted-commutative} treats reduced commutative algebras and compares the twisted derivations with the $\alpha$-derivations.
Most examples follow the result they illustrate. % claim-ok: factual claim about the paper

\section{Preliminaries and braided-symmetric derivations}\label{sec:sigma-derivations}

This section fixes the notation, recalls the classical notions that the introduction only names, and states the definition of a braided-symmetric derivation together with its value for the four structure-free involutions.

From here on, $A$ is a unital associative $K$-algebra.
The commutator, the Jordan product, the words nilpotent and reduced, and the Leibniz defect keep the meanings given in the introduction. % claim-ok: factual claim about this paper
We write $Z(A)$ for the centre of $A$, the set of elements $z$ with $zx = xz$ for all $x \in A$.
For a commutative algebra the word reduced is the notion of \citet[Definition 1.4.12]{ChambertLoir2021}, and Remark~\ref{rem:commutativity-needed} applies it to an algebra that is not commutative.

A derivation satisfies $D(1) = 0$, since $D(1) = D(1 \cdot 1) = 2D(1)$.
For $g \in A$, the map $\operatorname{ad}_g(x) = [g,x]$ is an \emph{inner derivation}.
With the convention $[g,x] = gx - xg$ of this paper, the principal derivations $x \mapsto xm - mx$ of \citet[\S9.2]{Weibel1994} are the maps $\operatorname{ad}_{-m}$.
We write $\Der(A)$ for the space of these maps, and $\Inn(A)$ for the space of the inner derivations.

The Jordan condition has a second form, which the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:twisted-semiprime} uses.
\citet[p.~1104]{Herstein1957} records that in rings of characteristic different from $2$ the condition $D(x \circ y) = D(x) \circ y + x \circ D(y)$ is equivalent to the square identity $D(x^2) = D(x)x + xD(x)$.
The two conditions agree in every characteristic. % claim-ok: a proof step (the linearization below)
Replacing $x$ by $x + y$ in the square identity and expanding gives the $\circ$ condition.
Setting $y = x$ in the $\circ$ condition gives $2D(x^2) = 2(D(x)x + xD(x))$, so the two differ only by the factor $2$.
They agree when $2$ is invertible in $K$, and also when $A$ is $2$-torsion-free, where the factor can be cancelled. % claim-ok: a proof step (cancelling 2 in torsion-free A)
We write $\JDer(A)$ for the space of linear maps satisfying the $\circ$ condition and use that form throughout.
In characteristic $2$ with $A$ commutative, $x \circ y = 0$, and every linear map satisfies the $\circ$ condition.

We write $\ADer(A)$ for the space of $\alpha$-derivations defined in the introduction. % claim-ok: notation for a notion already defined in the introduction

\begin{definition}\label{def:sigma-derivation} % ours: the term and the formalization are ours (ledger: own definition); the identity is stated in the introduction
A \emph{braided-symmetric derivation} for an involution $\sigma$ of $A \otimes A$ is a $K$-linear map $D \colon A \to A$ that satisfies identity~\eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz}.
The set $\BSDer_\sigma(A)$ is a $K$-subspace of $\mathrm{End}_K(A)$ by the linearity noted in the introduction.
For an automorphism or an anti-automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$ (both are defined in the introduction), we write $\BSDer_\sigma(A)^\alpha$ for the $K$-subspace of maps $D$ with $D\alpha = \alpha D$.
\end{definition}

Identity~\eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz} says that the following square commutes.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\mu_\sigma"] \arrow[d, "D \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes D"'] & A \arrow[d, "D"] \\
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\mu_\sigma"'] & A
\end{tikzcd}
\]

The name is close to an older one, and the next remark separates the two.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:distinction-from-braided}
Braided derivations \citep[\S2]{Rozanski1996} \citep[\S4.1, Definition 14]{Huru2006} form a different notion from braided-symmetric derivations, although both use a braiding.
Definition~\ref{def:sigma-derivation} asks instead for the ordinary Leibniz rule for the new product $\mu + \mu \circ \sigma$.

\end{remark}

Example~\ref{ex:not-associative} shows that the product $\cdot_{\sigma_\alpha}$ can fail to be associative and unital, even when $A$ is commutative.

\begin{example}\label{ex:not-associative}
Let $A = K \times K$ with componentwise operations, and let $\alpha(a,b) = (b,a)$.
Then $\alpha$ is an automorphism with $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$, and $\sigma_\alpha(x \otimes y) = \alpha(y) \otimes \alpha(x)$ is an involution of $A \otimes A$.
Since $A$ is commutative, $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(xy)$.
For $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $y = (y_1, y_2)$ this gives $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = (x_1y_1 + x_2y_2)(1,1)$.
Put $p = (1,0)$ and $q = (0,1)$.
Then $(p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} p) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} q = (1,1) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} q = (1,1)$, while $p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} (p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} q) = p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} 0 = 0$.
So $\cdot_{\sigma_\alpha}$ is not associative.
Every product $u \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y$ lies in $K(1,1)$, so $u \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} p \neq p$ for every $u$, and $\cdot_{\sigma_\alpha}$ has no unit.
\end{example}

The next remark records what Definition~\ref{def:sigma-derivation} says for the four structure-free involutions.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}
Let $A$ be a $K$-algebra and let $\sigma$ be a $K$-linear involution of $A \otimes A$.
Identity~\eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz} for $\sigma$ is the Leibniz rule for the product $\cdot_\sigma$.
So $\BSDer_\sigma(A)$ is the set of derivations of the nonassociative algebra $(A, \cdot_\sigma)$, in the sense of \citet[Part I]{Jacobson1937}.

For the identity, $x \cdot_{\mathrm{id}} y = 2xy$, so the identity reads
\[
2D(xy) = 2\bigl(D(x)y + xD(y)\bigr).
\]
If $\operatorname{char} K \neq 2$, we divide by $2$ and get $\BSDer_{\mathrm{id}}(A) = \Der(A)$.
If $\operatorname{char} K = 2$, then $x \cdot_{\mathrm{id}} y = 0$, so $\BSDer_{\mathrm{id}}(A)$ contains every linear map.
If $A \neq 0$, this is larger than $\Der(A)$: the linear map with $D(1) = 1$ and $D = 0$ on a complement of $K1$ is not a derivation. A derivation satisfies $D(1) = D(1 \cdot 1) = 2D(1) = 0$ in characteristic $2$.

For the swap $\tau$ we get $x \cdot_\tau y = xy + yx = x \circ y$.
So the defining identity is the $\circ$ form of the identity for a Jordan derivation recalled above.
Hence $\BSDer_\tau(A) = \JDer(A)$ in every characteristic.

Every derivation is a Jordan derivation \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
So in every characteristic
\[
\Der(A) \subseteq \BSDer_\tau(A) = \JDer(A).
\]
When $\operatorname{char} K \neq 2$, this gives $\BSDer_{\mathrm{id}}(A) \subseteq \BSDer_\tau(A)$.

The involution $-\mathrm{id}$ gives all linear endomorphisms, and $-\tau$ gives the Lie derivations. Their products are
\[
x \cdot_{-\mathrm{id}} y = xy - xy = 0,
\qquad
x \cdot_{-\tau} y = xy - yx = [x, y].
\]
So $\BSDer_{-\mathrm{id}}(A) = \mathrm{End}_K(A)$, since every linear map is a derivation of the zero product.
For $-\tau$ the product is $[x,y]$, and $\BSDer_{-\tau}(A)$ is the space of Lie derivations of $A$ \citep[p.~183]{Martindale1964}.
Its condition (2) is the Leibniz rule for this product. % claim-ok: condition (2) of Martindale 1964 is L([xy]) = [L(x), y] + [x, L(y)], which is the Leibniz rule for the product [x,y]
In characteristic $2$ the four involutions collapse to two, $-\mathrm{id} = \mathrm{id}$ and $-\tau = \tau$; when $\dim_K A \le 1$ they collapse further, since $\tau = \mathrm{id}$ on $K \otimes K$.
Remark~\ref{rem:natural-involutions} shows that in characteristic different from $2$ these are the only natural involutions of the functor $V \mapsto V \otimes V$.

\end{remark}

Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification} treated the four structure-free involutions. The next remark identifies them as the natural ones. % claim-ok: factual description of the plan

\begin{remark}\label{rem:natural-involutions}
The four entries of Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification} are the involutions of $A \otimes A$ given by natural transformations of the functor $V \mapsto V \otimes V$ on $K$-vector spaces. Here ``natural'' means natural for all $K$-linear maps and not only for algebra maps.

One way to see this is to identify the natural endomorphisms of the functor. % claim-ok: factual description of the argument
Such an endomorphism is determined by its value on $K^2$: every element of $V \otimes V$ is a sum of elements of the subspaces $j \otimes j(K^2 \otimes K^2)$ over the linear maps $j \colon K^2 \to V$. For $\dim V \le 1$ the assertion is immediate, since a nonzero $j \colon K^2 \to V$ is onto and hence so is $j \otimes j$.

Naturality in the maps $f \colon K^2 \to K^2$ says that this value, an endomorphism of $K^2 \otimes K^2$, commutes with every $f \otimes f$. % claim-ok: verified by the 2x2 matrix computation below
A direct computation with the $2 \times 2$ matrices shows that such endomorphisms are the linear combinations of $\mathrm{id}$ and $\tau$.

The maps $\mathrm{id}$ and $\tau$ are natural. % claim-ok: verified by direct computation below
Write a combination as $\lambda\,\mathrm{id} + \mu\tau$.
In characteristic different from $2$, being an involution reads $\lambda\mu = 0$ and $\lambda^2 + \mu^2 = 1$.
So the combination is one of the four entries above.

Every idempotent $P$ of $\mathrm{End}_K(A \otimes A)$ gives the involution $\mathrm{id} - 2P$, and when $2$ is invertible every involution arises this way, from $P = \tfrac12(\mathrm{id} - \sigma)$. The four structure-free idempotents are $0$, $\mathrm{id}$ and $\tfrac12(\mathrm{id} \pm \tau)$; their reflections are the four entries of Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}. When $2 \ne 0$ and $\dim_K A \ge 2$, an idempotent that uses the multiplication, such as $a \otimes b \mapsto ab \otimes 1$, gives an involution that is none of them.

In characteristic $2$ the maps $\lambda\,\mathrm{id} + (1+\lambda)\tau$, for $\lambda \in K$, are involutions as well, and they are the only natural ones. The product one of them defines is the scalar multiple $(1+\lambda)(x \circ y)$ of the Jordan product. So they add no class to the dictionary: for $1+\lambda \ne 0$ the Leibniz rule is the Jordan rule multiplied by a unit. For $1+\lambda = 0$ the product vanishes and every linear map qualifies. The value $\lambda = 1$ gives the zero product. % claim-ok: verified by direct computation in this remark

Involutions built from extra linear data on $A$ give other classes: for invertible $T$ the map $(T \otimes T^{-1}) \circ \tau$ is an involution. On $\mathbb{F}_5[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ with $T(1) = 1$ and $T(\varepsilon) = 1 + \varepsilon$ a direct computation on the basis pairs gives $\BSDer_\sigma(A) = \{0\}$, which is none of the four entries of Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}. % claim-ok: verified by direct computation (the basis-pair computation cited)
\end{remark}

\section{Twisted involutions and the Leibniz defect}\label{sec:twisted}

This section twists the swap by an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$, and treats the resulting class with one tool, the Leibniz defect of the introduction.

The words automorphism, anti-automorphism and involutive are defined in the introduction. % claim-ok: factual claim about this paper
By $(xy)^{T} = y^{T}x^{T}$, the transpose is an anti-automorphism of $M_n(K)$.

An involutive anti-automorphism is an involution of the ring $A$ in the sense of \citet[\S1.2]{Herstein1976}.
That is a map $*$ with $a^{**} = a$, $(a+b)^* = a^* + b^*$ and $(ab)^* = b^*a^*$.

Let $\alpha$ be involutive, of either kind, and put $\sigma_\alpha(x \otimes y) = \alpha(y) \otimes \alpha(x)$.
This map is the composite of the swap and $\alpha \otimes \alpha$, as the following diagram shows.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\tau"] \arrow[rr, bend left=40, "\sigma_\alpha"] & A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\alpha \otimes \alpha"] & A \otimes A
\end{tikzcd}
\]
Applying it twice gives
\[
x \otimes y \mapsto \alpha^2(x) \otimes \alpha^2(y),
\]
so $\sigma_\alpha^2 = \alpha^2 \otimes \alpha^2$, and $\sigma_\alpha$ is an involution of $A \otimes A$ exactly when $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$.

Indeed, if $\alpha^2 \otimes \alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$, then $\alpha^2 = \lambda\,\mathrm{id}$ with $\lambda^2 = 1$.
To see this, note that an endomorphism $\beta$ with $\beta \otimes \beta = \mathrm{id}$ is a scalar multiple of the identity.
Choose $y$ with $\beta(y) \ne 0$ and use the identity $\beta(x) \otimes \beta(y) = x \otimes y$. It forces $\beta(x) \in Kx$ for every $x$, with a scalar independent of $x$. % claim-ok: a proof step (the displayed tensor identity)
Then $\alpha^2(1) = 1$ gives $\lambda = 1$.

So $\sigma_\alpha$ defines a symmetrized product as in \eqref{eq:sigma-product}.
The product it defines is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twisted-product}
x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(y)\alpha(x).
\end{equation}
For $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$ the product $\mu_{\sigma_\alpha}$ is the Jordan product.
If $\alpha$ is an automorphism, then $\alpha(y)\alpha(x) = \alpha(yx)$.
If $\alpha$ is an anti-automorphism, then $\alpha(y)\alpha(x) = \alpha(xy)$.

The splitting of $A$ into the eigenspaces of $\alpha$ describes this product.
Put $A_\pm = \{x \in A : \alpha(x) = \pm x\}$.
We call the elements of $A_+$ \emph{symmetric} and the elements of $A_-$ \emph{skew}. % ours: standard algebraic definitions stated inline
The two sets make sense without a hypothesis on $2$. % claim-ok: immediate from the definition of A_+ and A_-
When $2$ is invertible, an involutive $\alpha$ splits $A = A_+ \oplus A_-$.
For $x \in A$,
\[
x = \tfrac12\bigl(x + \alpha(x)\bigr) + \tfrac12\bigl(x - \alpha(x)\bigr).
\]
The invertibility of $2$ is used only for the splitting, to see that the two sets span $A$ and that $A_+ \cap A_- = 0$.
In characteristic $2$ the two equations define the same set, so $A_+ = A_-$ is the fixed-point set of $\alpha$. % ours: standard algebraic definitions stated inline

The defect is the tool, and the next paragraph reads it in the Hochschild complex.
We recall the Hochschild complex of $A$ with coefficients in $A$ \citep[\S9.1]{Weibel1994}.
An \emph{$n$-cochain} is a $K$-multilinear map from $A^n$ to $A$.
So a $0$-cochain is an element of $A$, and a $1$-cochain is a linear map $D \colon A \to A$.
The \emph{coboundary} $\delta$ sends each $n$-cochain to an $(n+1)$-cochain, and $\delta \circ \delta = 0$ \citep[\S\S1.1 and 9.1]{Weibel1994}.
An \emph{$n$-cocycle} is an $n$-cochain $f$ with $\delta f = 0$.

On a $1$-cochain $D$ the coboundary is $(\delta D)(x,y) = xD(y) - D(xy) + D(x)y$ \citep[\S2, Definition 2.1]{Hochschild1945}.
So $E_D = -\delta D$.
Thus $D$ is a derivation exactly when $E_D = 0$, so derivations are the $1$-cocycles.
Since $\delta \circ \delta = 0$, the defect is a $2$-cocycle: for all $a, b, c \in A$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cocycle}
aE_D(b,c) - E_D(ab,c) + E_D(a,bc) - E_D(a,b)c = 0.
\end{equation}
This says that $E_D$ is a cocycle of the Hochschild complex: read the identity above as $\delta E_D = 0$ in the complex recalled in this paragraph. % claim-ok: the identity is delta E_D = 0, computed from the coboundary formula above

The defect also describes the other classes of Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}.
Expanding $D(x \circ y)$ shows that $D$ is a Jordan derivation exactly when $E_D(x,y) + E_D(y,x) = 0$ for all $x, y$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:defect}
Let $A$ be a unital associative $K$-algebra, let $\alpha$ be an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism of $A$, and let $D \colon A \to A$ be a linear map with $D\alpha = \alpha D$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $\alpha$ is an automorphism, then $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ if and only if $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(y,x)) = 0$ for all $x, y \in A$.
\item If $\alpha$ is an anti-automorphism, then $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ if and only if $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(x,y)) = 0$ for all $x, y \in A$.
\end{enumerate}
In both cases every derivation that commutes with $\alpha$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $E = E_D$.
By Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}, $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ means $D(x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y) = D(x) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y + x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} D(y)$ for all $x, y$.
We compute the difference of the two sides in each case.

For (i), the product~\eqref{eq:twisted-product} is $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(yx)$.
Since $D\alpha = \alpha D$, the left side is $D(xy) + \alpha(D(yx))$.
The right side is $D(x)y + \alpha(yD(x)) + xD(y) + \alpha(D(y)x)$.
We group the terms without $\alpha$ and the terms with $\alpha$.
The first group gives $D(xy) - D(x)y - xD(y) = E(x,y)$.
Since $\alpha$ is linear, the second group gives $\alpha\bigl(D(yx) - D(y)x - yD(x)\bigr) = \alpha(E(y,x))$.
So the left side minus the right side is $E(x,y) + \alpha(E(y,x))$, which proves (i).

The proof of (ii) follows the same steps as the proof of (i).
Here the product~\eqref{eq:twisted-product} is $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(xy)$.
Since $D\alpha = \alpha D$, the left side is
\[
D(xy) + \alpha(D(xy)),
\]
and the right side is
\[
D(x)y + \alpha(D(x)y) + xD(y) + \alpha(xD(y)).
\]
The same grouping gives the difference
\[
E(x,y) + \alpha(E(x,y)),
\]
which proves (ii).

If $D$ is a derivation, then $E = 0$, and both conditions hold.
\end{proof}

Without the hypothesis $D\alpha = \alpha D$ the same computation gives the residual identities
\[
D(x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y) - D(x) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y - x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} D(y)
= E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(y,x)) + (D\alpha - \alpha D)(yx)
\]
in case (i), and the same with $\alpha(E_D(x,y))$ and $(D\alpha - \alpha D)(xy)$ in case (ii).
So the hypothesis $D\alpha = \alpha D$ removes exactly the last term: with it, the two remaining terms vanish exactly when $D$ lies in the class, by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}.

Without it, they no longer characterize the class; when $A$ is unital they say something else. % claim-ok: verified by the computation of this remark
If they vanish, the difference of the two sides of the $\sigma_\alpha$-identity is $(D\alpha - \alpha D)(yx)$ in case (i) and $(D\alpha - \alpha D)(xy)$ in case (ii).
Since the products $yx$, and likewise $xy$, span a unital $A$, $D$ lies in the class exactly when $D\alpha = \alpha D$.

The computation of the two residual identities uses neither the unit of $A$ nor the involutivity of $\alpha$. Being involutive is what makes $\sigma_\alpha$ an involution, and it is also what makes the splitting $A = A_+ \oplus A_-$ above work. The unit of $A$ is used in the span argument of the preceding sentence. % claim-ok: verified by the computation just above

We now read the two conditions; the second needs no hypothesis, and for the first we assume that $2$ is invertible.

In case (ii), the equation $E_D + \alpha(E_D) = 0$ says $\alpha(E_D) = -E_D$.
So $E_D$ takes values in $A_-$, the skew elements of $\alpha$.
In case (i), we write $E_D = E_{\mathrm{s}} + E_{\mathrm{a}}$.
Here $E_{\mathrm{s}}(x,y) = \tfrac12(E_D(x,y) + E_D(y,x))$ is symmetric.
And $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) = \tfrac12(E_D(x,y) - E_D(y,x))$ is antisymmetric.

We swap $x$ and $y$ in the condition of (i).
Then we take the sum and the difference of the two equations.
So the condition holds exactly when $E_{\mathrm{s}}$ takes values in $A_-$ and $E_{\mathrm{a}}$ takes values in $A_+$.
Now let $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$.
Then $A_- = 0$ and $A_+ = A$, so the condition says $E_{\mathrm{s}} = 0$.
This is the Jordan condition, so Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i) recovers the swap case of Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:inner-twisted}
Let $\alpha$ be an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism of $A$, and let $D \in \Der(A)$.
Then $E_D = 0$.

By~\eqref{eq:twisted-product}, the two sides of the $\sigma_\alpha$-identity differ by
\[
D(\alpha(yx)) - \alpha(D(yx)),
\]
and for an anti-automorphism the same computation with $\alpha(xy)$ in place of $\alpha(yx)$ applies.

Since $A$ is unital, the products $yx$ span $A$.
So $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ if and only if $D\alpha = \alpha D$.

We now examine the inner derivations $\operatorname{ad}_g$ in this class. % claim-ok: factual description of the plan
The condition $\operatorname{ad}_g \alpha = \alpha \operatorname{ad}_g$ means
\[
[g - \alpha(g), \alpha(x)] = 0 \qquad \text{for all } x.
\]
Since $\alpha$ is bijective, this holds if and only if $g - \alpha(g) \in Z(A)$.
If $\alpha$ is an anti-automorphism instead, the same computation with $\alpha(x)\alpha(g)$ in place of $\alpha(xg)$ gives $[g + \alpha(g), \alpha(x)] = 0$ for all $x$, that is, $g + \alpha(g) \in Z(A)$.
\end{remark}

\citet[Corollary 5]{Cusack1975} and \citet[Theorem 1]{Bresar1988} proved that a Jordan derivation of a $2$-torsion-free semiprime ring is a derivation.

\begin{theorem}[{\citep[Theorem 1]{Bresar1988}}]\label{thm:bresar}
Let $R$ be a $2$-torsion-free semiprime ring. Then every Jordan derivation $D \colon R \to R$ is a derivation.
\end{theorem}

The next corollary applies Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar} to the twisted derivations.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:twisted-semiprime}
Let $A$ be a $2$-torsion-free semiprime $K$-algebra (over a field this means $\operatorname{char} K \ne 2$, or $A = 0$) with an involutive $K$-algebra automorphism or anti-automorphism $\alpha$.
Then $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha \cap \JDer(A) = \Der(A)^\alpha$, where $\Der(A)^\alpha$ is the set of derivations commuting with $\alpha$ (not to be confused with the space $\ADer(A)$ of $\alpha$-derivations of Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}, which is the notion the literature calls a twisted derivation).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Every derivation is a Jordan derivation by Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}.
With Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}, this gives $\Der(A)^\alpha \subseteq \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha \cap \JDer(A)$.

Conversely, every Jordan derivation of $A$ is a derivation by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}, which applies since a $K$-linear map is additive.
The $\circ$ form of the Jordan condition and the $x^2$ form of Herstein agree by the linearization of Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}, which uses the $2$-torsion-freeness assumed here.

So a map in the intersection is a derivation that commutes with $\alpha$.
\end{proof}

\section{Reduced commutative algebras and twisted derivations}\label{sec:twisted-commutative}

For commutative algebras the Jordan hypothesis in Corollary~\ref{cor:twisted-semiprime} can be dropped.

Recall from the introduction that a reduced commutative algebra has no nonzero nilpotent element. % claim-ok: recall of the definition of reduced in the introduction

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:twisted-commutative}
Let $A$ be a commutative reduced $K$-algebra with $2$ invertible in $K$, and let $\alpha$ be an involutive automorphism of $A$.
Then $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha = \Der(A)^\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every derivation commuting with $\alpha$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}.
Conversely, let $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
Write $E = E_D$.
Since $A$ is commutative, $E$ is symmetric.
So $E$ takes values in $A_-$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i).

Since $D$ commutes with $\alpha$, it maps each of $A_+$ and $A_-$ into itself; and since $\alpha$ is multiplicative, $A_iA_j \subseteq A_{i+j}$.
So if $x$ and $y$ lie in the same part, then $xy$, $D(x)y$ and $xD(y)$ all lie in $A_+$, and $E(x,y)$ lies in
\[
A_+ \cap A_- = 0.
\]

Now let $x \in A_+$ and $y \in A_-$, and put $z = E(x,y)$.
Then $z$ lies in $A_-$, and so do $xy$, $zx$ and $xz$, while $zy$ lies in $A_+$.
Apply the cocycle identity~\eqref{eq:cocycle} to $(a,b,c) = (z,x,y)$:
\[
zE(x,y) - E(zx,y) + E(z,xy) - E(z,x)y = 0.
\]
Both entries of the pairs $(zx,y)$ and $(z,xy)$ lie in $A_-$, so $E$ vanishes on them, and we get $z^2 = E(z,x)y$.

Apply~\eqref{eq:cocycle} to $(a,b,c) = (x,z,y)$:
\[
xE(z,y) - E(xz,y) + E(x,zy) - E(x,z)y = 0.
\]
Both entries of $(z,y)$ and of $(xz,y)$ lie in $A_-$, and both entries of $(x,zy)$ lie in $A_+$, so $E$ vanishes on all three and $E(x,z)y = 0$.
Since $E$ is symmetric, $z^2 = E(z,x)y = E(x,z)y = 0$.
So $z = 0$ because $A$ is reduced.

Since $E$ is bilinear and symmetric and $A = A_+ \oplus A_-$, we get $E = 0$.
Hence $D$ is a derivation.

The argument gives more than the statement.
It uses the commutativity of $A$ only to make $E_D$ symmetric, and the unit of $A$ nowhere.
Reducedness enters only to pass from $z \in A_-$ and $z^2 = 0$ to $z = 0$.
It therefore gives two stronger statements.
First, ``reduced'' may be replaced by ``no nonzero $z \in A_-$ satisfies $z^2 = 0$'', a condition that is empty when $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$.

Second, commutativity is used nowhere else in the argument.
Precisely, take any unital $K$-algebra $A$ with $2$ invertible and any involutive automorphism $\alpha$.
If $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ has symmetric defect $E_D$ and no nonzero $z \in A_-$ satisfies $z^2 = 0$, then $D$ is a derivation.
Remark~\ref{rem:commutativity-needed} shows that the conclusion fails when commutativity and reducedness are dropped together.
\end{proof}

Whether the hypotheses can be weakened is addressed after the examples below. % claim-ok: factual description of the plan

The next example shows that the hypotheses of the theorem can be met. % claim-ok: verified by the example that follows
Take $A = K[t]$, with $\alpha(t) = -t$ and $2 \ne 0$. Here $A$ is commutative and reduced.

The space $\Der(A)^\alpha$ is the set of derivations $f(t)\,d/dt$ with $f(-t) = -f(t)$, so $t\,d/dt$ is one of them. The theorem gives the equality $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha = \{f\,d/dt : f(-t) = -f(t)\}$.

A commutative reduced algebra has no nonzero nilpotent. A finite-dimensional one stays semisimple over every extension of the ground field, so it is a product of fields. % claim-ok: the definition of reduced (Section Preliminaries)

Over a perfect field a finite-dimensional algebra is separable exactly when it is semisimple \citep[\S10.7, Corollary~b]{Pierce1982}, so the fields of the product are finite separable extensions.

A separable extension has no nonzero $K$-derivation \citep[Lemma 9.2.1 and Theorem 9.2.11]{Weibel1994}. % claim-ok: the imperfect-field example of this section
So over a perfect field the instances with a nonzero derivation are infinite-dimensional. Over an imperfect field there are finite-dimensional ones. For odd $p$, with $K = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$, the polynomial $x^p - t$ is irreducible. So $L = K[x]/(x^p - t)$ is a field, and $A = L \times L$ is a commutative reduced $K$-algebra of dimension $2p$. The swap $\alpha(a,b) = (b,a)$ is an involutive automorphism, and $\Der(A)^\alpha$ is the diagonal of $\Der(L) \times \Der(L)$. This diagonal is nonzero because $D(x) = 1$, $D = 0$ on $K$, is a derivation of $L$.

Example~\ref{ex:twisted-nilpotent} shows that the reduced hypothesis cannot be dropped, and Example~\ref{ex:twisted-char-two} shows the same for the invertibility of $2$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:twisted-nilpotent}
Let $A = \mathbb{F}_5[u,v]/(u^2, v^2)$.
It has basis $\{1, u, v, uv\}$.
Let $\alpha$ fix $u$ and send $v$ to $-v$.
Let $D$ send $uv$ to $uv$ and the other three basis elements to $0$.
Then $D$ commutes with $\alpha$.
It satisfies the Leibniz rule for $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y$ on all basis pairs.
So $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
But $D$ is not a derivation, since
\[
D(uv) = uv \qquad \text{while} \qquad D(u)v + uD(v) = 0.
\]
A direct computation checks the three statements. Commuting with $\alpha$ is linear in $D$ and is checked on a basis. The Leibniz rule is linear in $D$ and bilinear in the pair, so it too is checked on basis pairs. The failure of the derivation rule is read at the single pair $(u,v)$.
Here $z = E(u,v) = uv$ is a nonzero element with $z^2 = 0$.
\end{example}

The next example shows that the invertibility of $2$ is needed in Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative}.

\begin{example}\label{ex:twisted-char-two}
Let $A = \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_2$, which is commutative and reduced, and let $\alpha$ swap the two factors.
Put $p = (1,0)$, $q = (0,1)$ and $e = (1,1)$, and let $D$ be the linear map with $D(p) = D(q) = e$, so that $D(e) = 0$.
Then $D$ commutes with $\alpha$.
On the basis pairs $(p,p)$, $(q,q)$ and $(p,q)$, both sides of the Leibniz rule for $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(xy)$ are zero.
Here $p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} p = e$, so the two sides vanish because $D(e) = 0$ and $2e = 0$, although $p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} p \ne 0$.
Since the rule is bilinear in the pair and $e = p + q$, it is enough to check the pairs with entries in $\{p, q, e\}$. For a pair with $e$ in one entry, $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} e = x + \alpha(x)$ lies in $\{0, e\}$, so the left side vanishes because $D(e) = D(0) = 0$. The right side is $D(x) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} e$, which vanishes because $D(x)$ lies in $\{0, e\}$ and $e \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} e = 2e = 0$.
So $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
But $D$ is not a derivation: since $p^2 = p$, a derivation would satisfy $D(p) = D(p^2) = 2pD(p) = 0$, while $D(p) = e$.
\end{example}

One may ask whether the twisted derivations are the $\alpha$-derivations. % claim-ok: factual description of the question
The $\alpha$-derivations were defined for automorphisms in Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}.
They differ from $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ in both directions, and one algebra shows both failures.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twisted}
Let $A = \mathbb{F}_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^3)$, and let $\alpha$ be the automorphism with $\alpha(\varepsilon) = -\varepsilon$.
Then
\[
\ADer(A) \not\subseteq \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha \not\subseteq \ADer(A).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The algebra $A$ has basis $\{1, \varepsilon, \varepsilon^2\}$.
The map $\alpha$ fixes $1$ and $\varepsilon^2$ and sends $\varepsilon$ to $-\varepsilon$.
So it is an involutive automorphism with $A_+ = \mathbb{F}_3 1 + \mathbb{F}_3\varepsilon^2$ and $A_- = \mathbb{F}_3\varepsilon$.
Since $A$ is commutative, $E_D$ is symmetric for every linear map $D$.
Let $D$ be a linear map with $D\alpha = \alpha D$.
Since $E_D$ is symmetric, the condition $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(y,x)) = 0$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i) reads $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(x,y)) = 0$, that is, $E_D$ takes values in $A_-$; so $D$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ exactly when this holds.
We give one map for each non-inclusion.
Both maps are diagonal on the basis, so both commute with $\alpha$.

For the first non-inclusion, let $D(a + b\varepsilon + c\varepsilon^2) = b\varepsilon + c\varepsilon^2$.
We check that $D$ is an $\alpha$-derivation on pairs of basis elements.
For a pair that contains $1$, both sides equal $D$ of the other element, since $D(1) = 0$ and $\alpha(1) = 1$.
For the pair $(\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, the left side is $D(\varepsilon^2) = \varepsilon^2$.
The right side is $D(\varepsilon)\alpha(\varepsilon) + \alpha(\varepsilon)D(\varepsilon) = -2\varepsilon^2 = \varepsilon^2$.

The remaining pairs have product $0$, and each term on the right side is a multiple of $\varepsilon^3 = 0$.
So $D \in \ADer(A)$.
On the other hand, $E_D(\varepsilon, \varepsilon) = D(\varepsilon^2) - 2\varepsilon D(\varepsilon) = \varepsilon^2 - 2\varepsilon^2 = 2\varepsilon^2$.
This element is nonzero and lies in $A_+$.
So $E_D$ does not take values in $A_-$, and $D \notin \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.

The second non-inclusion uses a derivation.

Let $d$ be the linear map
\[
d(a + b\varepsilon + c\varepsilon^2) = b\varepsilon + 2c\varepsilon^2.
\]
It is a derivation, since $d(1) = 0$, $d(\varepsilon) = \varepsilon$, $d(\varepsilon^2) = 2\varepsilon^2$, and exponents add under multiplication.
So $E_d = 0$, and $d \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
On the other hand,
\[
d(\varepsilon^2) - d(\varepsilon)\alpha(\varepsilon) - \alpha(\varepsilon)d(\varepsilon) = 2\varepsilon^2 + 2\varepsilon^2 = \varepsilon^2 \neq 0.
\]
Hence $d \notin \ADer(A)$.
\end{proof}

The two classes can also meet, and one of them can fail to be defined. Part (b) of Example~\ref{ex:twisted-meet} shows the first, part (a) the second. % claim-ok: verified by Example~\ref{ex:twisted-meet} below

\begin{example}\label{ex:twisted-meet}
(a) Let $A = M_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$ and let $\alpha$ be the transpose, an involutive anti-automorphism.
Then $\ADer(A)$ is not defined, since it needs an automorphism, so this part gives a twisted derivation with no $\alpha$-derivation to compare with.
Take $g = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is nonzero and skew for the transpose, and let $\operatorname{ad}_g$ be the inner derivation $x \mapsto [g,x]$.
It commutes with $\alpha$ by the anti-automorphism case of Remark~\ref{rem:inner-twisted}, since $g + \alpha(g) = 0$, and it has $E = 0$, so it lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}.

(b) Now let $A = K[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ with $\alpha(\varepsilon) = -\varepsilon$.
The map $D(a + b\varepsilon) = b\varepsilon$ is a derivation and an $\alpha$-derivation, and it commutes with $\alpha$.
So it lies in both $\ADer(A)$ and $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}.
A direct computation gives that when $\operatorname{char} K \neq 2$ both classes are one-dimensional, spanned by $\varepsilon \mapsto \varepsilon$, so here they coincide. When $\operatorname{char} K = 2$ the map $\alpha$ is the identity, $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ is the whole of $\mathrm{End}_K(A)$, and $\ADer(A) = \Der(A)$ is two-dimensional.
\end{example}

Example~\ref{ex:twisted-meet} shows that the two classes can meet; the remark below examines which hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} are required.

\begin{remark}[Reducedness and commutativity cannot both be dropped]\label{rem:commutativity-needed}
Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} fails when the hypotheses that $A$ is commutative and that $A$ is reduced are both dropped.

Let $A$ be the four-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_3$-algebra with basis $1, t, v, w$. In it $1$ is a unit and
\[
t^2 = 0, \qquad tv = vt = tw = wt = 0, \qquad v^2 = w^2 = wv = t, \qquad vw = 0,
\]
and let $\alpha$ be the linear map with $\alpha(1) = 1$, $\alpha(t) = t$, $\alpha(v) = -v$ and $\alpha(w) = -w$.

Then $A$ is associative, a check on the $64$ three-element products $x(yz) = (xy)z$ of basis elements, and $\alpha$ is an involutive automorphism.

The linear map $D$ with $D(v) = v + w$ and $D(1) = D(t) = D(w) = 0$ commutes with $\alpha$ and satisfies the Leibniz rule for $\cdot_{\sigma_\alpha}$ on every pair of basis elements. That rule is linear in $D$ and bilinear in the pair, so $D$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.

It is not a derivation, since $D(vw) = 0$ while $D(v)w + vD(w) = vw + w^2 = t \neq 0$.
Solving the linear conditions on the basis pairs gives $\dim \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha = 3$ and $\dim \Der(A)^\alpha = 2$.

The defect is antisymmetric at $(v,w)$, where $E_D(v,w) = -t$. Also, $A_-$ does contain a nonzero square-zero element, $(v+w)^2 = 0$. This is consistent with the symmetric-defect statement at the end of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative}.

The algebra is neither commutative ($vw = 0$ while $wv = t$) nor reduced ($t \ne 0$ and $t^2 = 0$), so the example does not show that commutativity alone is needed, and we make no such claim.

One may ask whether a witness that drops commutativity while keeping reducedness exists in finite dimension over a finite field. % claim-ok: literature search recorded in ledger/projects/braided-sym-deriv/state.md
The radical of a finite-dimensional algebra is nilpotent \citep[\S4.4]{Pierce1982}, hence zero in a reduced algebra, so the algebra is semisimple.
By the structure theorem of Wedderburn it is then a product of matrix algebras over division rings \citep[\S3.5]{Pierce1982}. Reducedness forces the matrix sizes to be $1$, since $e_{12}^2 = 0$ in $M_n(D)$ for $n \geq 2$.

So the candidate algebra is a product of division rings. Over a finite base field each factor is a field, by the little theorem of Wedderburn \citep[Exercise 4.9.19]{ChambertLoir2021}.

The simplest non-commutative candidate is thus a division algebra such as the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$. % claim-ok: the Wedderburn argument of this paragraph
At $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$ we have $\sigma_\alpha = \tau$, so the assertion becomes $\JDer(\mathbb{H}) = \Der(\mathbb{H})$, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}.

The quaternions do not provide a witness either; the computations below show this directly. % claim-ok: verified by the two-dimensional computations shown below
Every automorphism of $\mathbb{H}$ is inner \citep[\S12.6]{Pierce1982}.
So the involutive ones are the identity and the maps $x \mapsto qxq^{-1}$ by pure imaginary quaternions.

Any two of the latter are conjugate when $q$ and $q'$ are pure imaginary of norm $1$. % claim-ok: verified by the norm computation below
To see this, take $q' \ne -q$ and put $u = 1 - q'q$; then $u q u^{-1} = q'$.
The element $u$ is nonzero because $u\bar{u} = 2 - (qq' + q'q)$.
Indeed, $qq' + q'q = 2$ would give $(q + q')^2 = 0$, hence $q' = -q$.
For $q' = -q$ the two maps induce the same conjugation.

The structure constants of $\mathbb{H}$ are rational, so one computation settles all of these conjugations at once.
For a conjugation $\alpha$, write $D$ on the basis and read the twisted Leibniz rule as linear conditions on the sixteen coordinates of $D$.
Solving these conditions gives $\dim\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(\mathbb{H})^\alpha = \dim\Der(\mathbb{H})^\alpha = 1$.

For $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$ the same computation gives $3$ for both, and the two classes agree by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}.

So the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} holds at $\mathbb{H}$ for every involutive $\alpha$, although $\mathbb{H}$ is not commutative.
The same computation gives $\dim\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(\mathbb{H}) = 1 < 3 = \dim\Der(\mathbb{H})$ for $\alpha \ne \mathrm{id}$: at $\mathbb{H}$ the derivations lie in the twisted class only in so far as they commute with $\alpha$.

For a general reduced algebra with $2$ invertible and $\alpha$ an involutive automorphism, the argument of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} reduces the question to the skew part.

Let $E = E_D$ and $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) = E(x,y) - E(y,x)$, twice the antisymmetric part of Section~\ref{sec:twisted}.
Then $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) = D([x,y]) - [D(x),y] - [x,D(y)]$.
So $E_{\mathrm{a}} = 0$ says that $D$ is a derivation for the commutator bracket.

Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i), applied at $(x,y)$ and at $(y,x)$, gives $\alpha(E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y)) = E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y)$.
So $E_{\mathrm{a}}$ takes values in $A_+$.

Since $\alpha$ is multiplicative and $D$ commutes with it, $E$ maps $A_i \times A_j$ into $A_{i+j}$.
Since $E$ is additive in each argument and $A = A_+ + A_-$, three cases remain.

If $x \in A_+$ and $y \in A_-$ then $E(x,y)$ and $E(y,x)$ lie in $A_-$, so $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) \in A_+ \cap A_- = 0$.
If $x, y \in A_+$ then $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + yx$, so the restriction of $D$ to $A_+$ is a Jordan derivation of $A_+$. The ring $A_+$ is reduced, hence semiprime, and is $2$-torsion-free because $2$ is invertible in $K$, so $D$ is a derivation there by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}; hence $E(x,y) = 0$.

So only the case $x, y \in A_-$ remains.
There $E$ is alternating: its values lie in $A_+$ because $E$ maps $A_- \times A_-$ into $A_+$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i) at $(x,x)$ puts $E(x,x)$ in $A_-$.
Hence $E(x,x) \in A_+ \cap A_- = 0$.

If $E$ vanishes on $A_- \times A_-$, then $E_{\mathrm{a}} = 0$ and the defect is symmetric. % claim-ok: the open point of this remark
The argument at the end of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} then makes $D$ a derivation.
Whether $E$ must vanish there is what is missing, and it is not settled here.

The three conditions are equivalent, as the computation below shows. % claim-ok: the equivalence is proven by the argument that follows
Write (i) for $D \in \Der(A)$, (ii) for $E = 0$ on $A_+ \times A_-$, and (iii) for $E = 0$ on $A_- \times A_-$.
A derivation has $E = 0$, so (i) implies (ii) and (iii).

Assume (iii).
Then $E$ vanishes on $A_+ \times A_+$ by the argument of the case $x, y \in A_+$ above, and on $A_- \times A_-$ by hypothesis. On a mixed pair $E(x,y) = E(y,x)$, because both lie in $A_-$ and their difference lies in $A_+$.
So $E$ is symmetric. The argument of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative}, which uses only the symmetry of $E$ and not the commutativity of $A$, gives $E = 0$; so $D$ is a derivation.

Assume (ii).
Then $E$ vanishes on $A_+ \times A_+$ as above, and on $A_+ \times A_-$ by hypothesis.

On $A_- \times A_+$ it vanishes for the following reason.
For $x \in A_-$ and $y \in A_+$, the element $E(x,y)$ lies in $A_+$ because $E$ maps $A_- \times A_+$ into $A_+$.
The difference $E(x,y) - E(y,x) = E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y)$ lies in $A_+$ by the paragraph above, and in $A_-$ because its two terms lie in $A_-$.
Since $E(y,x) = 0$ by hypothesis, this forces $E(x,y) = 0$.

On $A_- \times A_-$ the defect $E$ is alternating, hence antisymmetric: $E(x,x)$ lies in $A_+$ because $E$ maps $A_- \times A_-$ into $A_+$, and in $A_-$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i) at $(x,x)$.

So $E$ is antisymmetric, which makes $D$ a Jordan derivation of $A$.
Since $A$ is reduced and $2$ is invertible in $K$, $A$ is semiprime and $2$-torsion-free.
The two forms of the Jordan condition agree by the linearization of Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}, so $D$ is a derivation by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}.

Thus (i), (ii) and (iii) are equivalent, and a counterexample to the conclusion for reduced $A$ has a nonzero defect on the mixed pairs as well as on $A_- \times A_-$. The example above, where $E$ vanishes on the mixed pairs, escapes this because its algebra is not reduced.
\end{remark}
