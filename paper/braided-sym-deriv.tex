\begin{abstract}
We study braided-symmetric derivations of an algebra over a field.
These are maps that satisfy the Leibniz rule for the product symmetrized by an involution of $A \otimes A$.
The choice of involution decides which derivations appear.
The identity involution gives the ordinary derivations when $2$ is invertible.
The swap gives the Jordan derivations.
The negative of the identity gives all linear endomorphisms, and the negative of the swap gives the Lie derivations.
We then twist the swap by an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism.
The Leibniz defect of a map decides when it is a twisted derivation.
For a commutative reduced algebra with $2$ invertible, the twisted maps that commute with the automorphism are exactly the derivations that commute with it.
On the algebra $\mathbb{F}_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^3)$ the twisted derivations and the $\alpha$-derivations do not contain each other.
An example with nilpotent elements shows that the reduced hypothesis is needed, an example in characteristic $2$ shows that the invertibility of $2$ is needed, and a four-dimensional example shows that reducedness and commutativity cannot be dropped together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Throughout, $K$ is a field and an algebra means a unital associative $K$-algebra.
We take all maps between algebras to be $K$-linear.
We say so when a result cited below holds more generally for additive maps on rings.

A \emph{derivation} of an algebra $A$ is a linear map $D \colon A \to A$ with $D(xy) = D(x)y + xD(y)$ for all $x, y \in A$.
The notion goes back to \citet[Part I]{Jacobson1937}, who studied the derivations of an arbitrary nonassociative algebra.
\citet[\S\S2 and 4]{Hochschild1945} defined the cocycles of an associative algebra, and its $1$-cocycles are the derivations.
A \emph{Lie derivation} is a linear map with $D([x,y]) = [D(x), y] + [x, D(y)]$ \citep[p.~183]{Martindale1964}.
\citet[\S1]{Benkovic2015} treats derivations, Jordan derivations and Lie derivations as $f$-derivations, for $f = x_1x_2$, $x_1 \circ x_2$ and $[x_1, x_2]$, so that one identity for a multilinear polynomial $f$ covers all three.
\citet[Theorem 4.3]{BeidarBresarChebotarMartindale2001} studied maps that act as derivations on a fixed multilinear polynomial, earlier than Benkovi\v{c}.
A \emph{Jordan derivation} of $A$ is a linear map with $D(x \circ y) = D(x) \circ y + x \circ D(y)$ for all $x, y \in A$, where $x \circ y = xy + yx$.
The sum $xy + yx$ is twice the Jordan product $\tfrac12 (xy + yx)$ of an associative algebra \citep[Example 2.1]{BresarZelmanov2025}.
Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner form a product $ab$ from the two products $a \times b$ and $b \times a$ of an associative system, and call this quasi-multiplication \citep[p.~64]{JordanVonNeumannWigner1934}.
\citet[p.~1104]{Herstein1957} takes the sum $ab + ba$ without the half.
We follow Herstein and do not divide by $2$, so that characteristic $2$ is allowed.
The results below need three notions for a ring $R$.
We call $R$ \emph{prime} if $aRb = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$ \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
We call $R$ \emph{semiprime} if $aRa = 0$ implies $a = 0$ \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
We call $R$ \emph{$2$-torsion-free} if $2a = 0$ implies $a = 0$ \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
So every prime ring is semiprime.
Herstein proved that every Jordan derivation of a prime ring of characteristic different from $2$ is a derivation \citep[Theorem 3.1]{Herstein1957}.
Every derivation is a Jordan derivation, and the converse is false in general \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
\citet[Corollary 5]{Cusack1975} extended this result of Herstein to $2$-torsion-free semiprime rings, and \citet[Theorem 1]{Bresar1988} gave an independent proof.

Let $A$ be an algebra over $K$ with product $\mu$.
Let $\sigma \colon A \otimes A \to A \otimes A$ be a $K$-linear map with $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{id}$, for instance the swap $\tau(x \otimes y) = y \otimes x$.
It gives a symmetrized product, as the Jordan product does \citep[Example 2.1]{BresarZelmanov2025}
\[
x \cdot_\sigma y = \mu(x \otimes y) + \mu(\sigma(x \otimes y)).
\]

For $\sigma = \tau$ this is the Jordan product $x \circ y$.
Put $\mu_\sigma = \mu + \mu \circ \sigma$.
Since $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{id}$, we get $\mu_\sigma \circ \sigma = \mu_\sigma$, that is, the following triangle commutes.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\sigma"] \arrow[rd, "\mu_\sigma"'] & A \otimes A \arrow[d, "\mu_\sigma"] \\
& A
\end{tikzcd}
\]
Example~\ref{ex:not-associative} shows that a twisted version of this product can fail to be associative and to have a unit.

We call a linear map $D \colon A \to A$ a \emph{braided-symmetric derivation} for $\sigma$ if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma-leibniz}
D(x \cdot_\sigma y) = D(x) \cdot_\sigma y + x \cdot_\sigma D(y).
\end{equation}
So \eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz} is the Leibniz rule for the symmetrized product.
Other authors have studied \emph{braided derivations}, which use a braiding operator directly \citep[\S2]{Rozanski1996} \citep[\S4.1, Definition 14]{Huru2006}.
Remark~\ref{rem:distinction-from-braided} compares the two identities.
Our identity uses only $\mu$, $\sigma$ and $D$.
We use the word \emph{braided} by analogy with these braiding operators: no braided monoidal category is involved, and $\sigma$ is any $K$-linear involution of $A \otimes A$.

We now describe the main results.
Our first step is a dictionary.
Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification} shows that the identity gives exactly the derivations when $2$ is invertible, the swap gives exactly the Jordan derivations, and the two sign changes give all linear endomorphisms and the Lie derivations.

Our second step twists the swap by an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$.
For an involutive $\alpha$ we put $\sigma_\alpha(x \otimes y) = \alpha(y) \otimes \alpha(x)$, and we write $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ for the maps of the class of Definition~\ref{def:sigma-derivation} that commute with $\alpha$; we call them \emph{twisted derivations}.
Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} describes the twisted derivations through the defect $E_D(x,y) = D(xy) - D(x)y - xD(y)$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:twisted-semiprime} shows that, on $2$-torsion-free semiprime algebras, the Jordan maps among the twisted derivations are the derivations commuting with $\alpha$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} treats commutative reduced algebras and needs no Jordan hypothesis.
Example~\ref{ex:twisted-nilpotent} shows that the reduced hypothesis is needed, and Example~\ref{ex:twisted-char-two} shows the same for the invertibility of $2$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:twisted} shows that the twisted derivations and the $\alpha$-derivations on one algebra do not contain each other.
Remark~\ref{rem:commutativity-needed} shows that Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} fails when both of its hypotheses are dropped, and Appendix~\ref{app:positive} proves its conclusion for four classes of non-commutative algebras.

We follow this order.
Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations} recalls the classical notions, defines braided-symmetric derivations and treats the four structure-free involutions.
Section~\ref{sec:twisted} treats the twisted swap through the Leibniz defect.
Section~\ref{sec:twisted-commutative} treats reduced commutative algebras and compares the twisted derivations with the $\alpha$-derivations.
We place each example right after the result it illustrates.

\section{Preliminaries and braided-symmetric derivations}\label{sec:sigma-derivations}

This section recalls the classical notions we need, defines braided-symmetric derivations and identifies them for the four structure-free involutions.

From now on, $A$ is a $K$-algebra with unit $1$.
We write $[x,y] = xy - yx$ for the commutator, $x \circ y = xy + yx$ for the Jordan product, and $Z(A)$ for the centre.
The centre is the set of elements $z$ with $zx = xz$ for all $x \in A$.
We call an element $x$ \emph{nilpotent} if $x^n = 0$ for some $n \geq 1$.
A commutative algebra is \emph{reduced} if its only nilpotent element is zero \citep[1.4.12]{ChambertLoir2021}.

A derivation is defined in the introduction.
Every derivation satisfies $D(1) = 0$, since $D(1) = D(1 \cdot 1) = 2D(1)$.
For $g \in A$, the map $\operatorname{ad}_g(x) = [g,x]$ is an \emph{inner derivation}, called a principal derivation by \citet[\S9.2]{Weibel1994}.
We write $\Der(A)$ and $\Inn(A)$ for these two spaces.

We add a word on the characteristic for Jordan derivations.
\citet[p.~1104]{Herstein1957} defines a \emph{Jordan derivation} as a derivation of the Jordan ring of $A$, that is by $D(x \circ y) = D(x) \circ y + x \circ D(y)$, and records that for rings of characteristic different from $2$ it can be defined by the square identity $D(x^2) = D(x)x + xD(x)$.
We write $\JDer(A)$ for the space of linear maps with $D(x \circ y) = D(x) \circ y + x \circ D(y)$ for all $x, y$.
Replacing $x$ by $x + y$ gives the condition $D(x \circ y) = D(x) \circ y + x \circ D(y)$ in every characteristic.
Setting $y = x$ in this condition gives $2D(x^2) = 2(D(x)x + xD(x))$.
So the two conditions agree when $2$ is invertible in $K$, and also when $A$ is $2$-torsion-free, where the factor $2$ in the displayed identity can be cancelled; we use the $\circ$ form throughout.
When $\operatorname{char} K = 2$ and $A$ is commutative, $x \circ y = 0$, and every linear map satisfies the $\circ$ condition.

For an automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$, an \emph{$\alpha$-derivation} satisfies $D(xy) = D(x)\alpha(y) + \alpha(x)D(y)$ \citep[\S1]{HosseiniKarizaki2019}.
We write $\ADer(A)$ for this space.

We now compare the Jordan derivations with the derivations.
Every derivation is a Jordan derivation \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}, so $\Der(A) \subseteq \JDer(A)$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:sigma-derivation}
Let $A$ be a $K$-algebra and let $\sigma$ be a $K$-linear involution of $A \otimes A$.
A \emph{braided-symmetric derivation} for $\sigma$ is a $K$-linear map $D \colon A \to A$ that satisfies identity~\eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz}, that is, $D(x \cdot_\sigma y) = D(x) \cdot_\sigma y + x \cdot_\sigma D(y)$ for all $x, y \in A$.
We write $\BSDer_\sigma(A)$ for the set of braided-symmetric derivations for $\sigma$.
This set is a $K$-subspace of $\mathrm{End}_K(A)$, since the condition is linear in $D$.
For an automorphism or an anti-automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$ (both are recalled in Section~\ref{sec:twisted}), we write $\BSDer_\sigma(A)^\alpha$ for the subset of maps $D$ with $D\alpha = \alpha D$.
\end{definition}

Identity~\eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz} says that the following square commutes.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\mu_\sigma"] \arrow[d, "D \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes D"'] & A \arrow[d, "D"] \\
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\mu_\sigma"'] & A
\end{tikzcd}
\]

The name is close to an older one, and the next remark separates the two.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:distinction-from-braided}
Braided derivations \citep[\S2]{Rozanski1996} \citep[\S4.1, Definition 14]{Huru2006} form a different notion from braided-symmetric derivations, although both use a braiding.
Definition~\ref{def:sigma-derivation} asks instead for the ordinary Leibniz rule for the new product $\mu + \mu \circ \sigma$.

\end{remark}

Example~\ref{ex:not-associative} shows that the product $\cdot_\sigma$ can fail to be associative and unital, even when $A$ is commutative.

\begin{example}\label{ex:not-associative}
Let $A = K \times K$ with componentwise operations, and let $\alpha(a,b) = (b,a)$.
Then $\alpha$ is an automorphism with $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$, and $\sigma_\alpha(x \otimes y) = \alpha(y) \otimes \alpha(x)$ is an involution of $A \otimes A$.
Since $A$ is commutative, $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(xy)$.
For $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $y = (y_1, y_2)$ this gives $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = (x_1y_1 + x_2y_2)(1,1)$.
Put $p = (1,0)$ and $q = (0,1)$.
Then $(p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} p) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} q = (1,1) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} q = (1,1)$, while $p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} (p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} q) = p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} 0 = 0$.
So $\cdot_{\sigma_\alpha}$ is not associative.
Every product $u \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y$ lies in $K(1,1)$, so $u \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} p \neq p$ for every $u$, and $\cdot_{\sigma_\alpha}$ has no unit.
\end{example}

The next remark records what Definition~\ref{def:sigma-derivation} says for the four structure-free involutions.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}
Let $A$ be a $K$-algebra and let $\sigma$ be a $K$-linear involution of $A \otimes A$.
Identity~\eqref{eq:sigma-leibniz} for $\sigma$ is the Leibniz rule for the product $\cdot_\sigma$.
So $\BSDer_\sigma(A)$ is the set of derivations of the nonassociative algebra $(A, \cdot_\sigma)$, in the sense of \citet[Part I]{Jacobson1937}.

For the identity, $x \cdot_{\mathrm{id}} y = 2xy$, so the identity reads
\[
2D(xy) = 2\bigl(D(x)y + xD(y)\bigr).
\]
If $\operatorname{char} K \neq 2$, we divide by $2$ and get $\BSDer_{\mathrm{id}}(A) = \Der(A)$.
If $\operatorname{char} K = 2$, then $x \cdot_{\mathrm{id}} y = 0$, so $\BSDer_{\mathrm{id}}(A)$ contains every linear map.
If $A \neq 0$, this is larger than $\Der(A)$: the linear map with $D(1) = 1$ and $D = 0$ on a complement of $K1$ is not a derivation, since a derivation satisfies $D(1) = D(1 \cdot 1) = 2D(1) = 0$ in characteristic $2$.

For the swap $\tau$ we get $x \cdot_\tau y = xy + yx = x \circ y$.
So the defining identity is the $\circ$ form of the identity for a Jordan derivation recalled above.
Hence $\BSDer_\tau(A) = \JDer(A)$ in every characteristic.

Every derivation is a Jordan derivation \citep[p.~1003]{Bresar1988}.
So in every characteristic
\[
\Der(A) \subseteq \BSDer_\tau(A) = \JDer(A).
\]
When $\operatorname{char} K \neq 2$, this gives $\BSDer_{\mathrm{id}}(A) \subseteq \BSDer_\tau(A)$.

The involutions $-\mathrm{id}$ and $-\tau$ give the two remaining entries of this dictionary.
Their products are
\[
x \cdot_{-\mathrm{id}} y = xy - xy = 0,
\qquad
x \cdot_{-\tau} y = xy - yx = [x, y].
\]
So $\BSDer_{-\mathrm{id}}(A) = \mathrm{End}_K(A)$, since every linear map is a derivation of the zero product, and $\BSDer_{-\tau}(A)$ is the space of Lie derivations of $A$ \citep[p.~183]{Martindale1964}.
In characteristic $2$ the four involutions collapse to two, $-\mathrm{id} = \mathrm{id}$ and $-\tau = \tau$; when $\dim_K A \le 1$ they collapse further, since $\tau = \mathrm{id}$ on $K \otimes K$.

These four are the involutions of $A \otimes A$ given by natural transformations of the functor $V \mapsto V \otimes V$ on $K$-vector spaces, that is, natural for all $K$-linear maps and not only for algebra maps; the natural endomorphisms of the functor are the linear combinations of $\mathrm{id}$ and $\tau$, for three reasons: such an endomorphism is determined by its value on $K^2$, since every element of $V \otimes V$ is a sum of elements of the subspaces $j \otimes j(K^2 \otimes K^2)$ over the linear maps $j \colon K^2 \to V$, and $\dim V \le 1$ is immediate from the same naturality in $K \to K^2 \to K$; naturality in the maps $f \colon K^2 \to K^2$ makes that value an endomorphism of $K^2 \otimes K^2$ commuting with every $f \otimes f$, and those are the linear combinations of $\mathrm{id}$ and $\tau$ by a direct computation with the $2 \times 2$ matrices; and $\mathrm{id}$ and $\tau$ are natural. In characteristic different from $2$ an involution among them satisfies $\lambda\mu = 0$ and $\lambda^2 + \mu^2 = 1$, so it is one of the four.
Every idempotent $P$ of $\mathrm{End}_K(A \otimes A)$ gives the involution $\mathrm{id} - 2P$, and when $2$ is invertible every involution arises this way, from $P = \tfrac12(\mathrm{id} - \sigma)$; the four structure-free idempotents are $0$, $\mathrm{id}$ and $\tfrac12(\mathrm{id} \pm \tau)$, whose reflections are the four entries above, while, when $2 \ne 0$ and $\dim_K A \ge 2$, an idempotent that uses the multiplication, such as $a \otimes b \mapsto ab \otimes 1$, gives an involution that is none of them.
In characteristic $2$ the maps $\lambda\,\mathrm{id} + (1+\lambda)\tau$, for $\lambda \in K$, are involutions as well, and they are the only natural ones; the product one of them defines is the scalar multiple $(1+\lambda)(x \circ y)$ of the Jordan product, so they add no class to the dictionary, and the value $\lambda = 1$ gives the zero product.
Involutions that use structure of $A$ give other classes: for invertible $T$ the map $(T \otimes T^{-1}) \circ \tau$ is an involution, and on $\mathbb{F}_5[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ with $T(1) = 1$ and $T(\varepsilon) = 1 + \varepsilon$ a direct computation on the two basis elements gives $\BSDer_\sigma(A) = \{0\}$, which is none of the four entries above.
\end{remark}

\section{Twisted involutions and the Leibniz defect}\label{sec:twisted}

Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification} treated the four structure-free involutions.
This section twists the swap by an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism $\alpha$ of $A$ and treats it with one tool, the Leibniz defect.

We recall the notions used here.
An \emph{automorphism} of $A$ is a bijective $K$-linear map $\alpha \colon A \to A$ with $\alpha(xy) = \alpha(x)\alpha(y)$ and $\alpha(1) = 1$.
An \emph{anti-automorphism} of $A$ is a bijective $K$-linear map $\alpha \colon A \to A$ with $\alpha(xy) = \alpha(y)\alpha(x)$ and $\alpha(1) = 1$.
By $(xy)^{T} = y^{T}x^{T}$, the transpose is an anti-automorphism of $M_n(K)$.
In both cases $\alpha$ is \emph{involutive} if $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$.
An involutive anti-automorphism is an involution of the ring $A$ in the sense of \citet[\S1.2]{Herstein1976}, that is, a map $*$ with $a^{**} = a$, $(a+b)^* = a^* + b^*$ and $(ab)^* = b^*a^*$.

We need $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$ for the following reason.
Applying $\sigma_\alpha$ below twice gives
\[
x \otimes y \mapsto \alpha^2(x) \otimes \alpha^2(y),
\]
so $\sigma_\alpha^2 = \alpha^2 \otimes \alpha^2$, and $\sigma_\alpha$ is an involution of $A \otimes A$ exactly when $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$: if $\alpha^2 \otimes \alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$, then $\alpha^2 = \lambda\,\mathrm{id}$ with $\lambda^2 = 1$, and $\alpha^2(1) = 1$ gives $\lambda = 1$.
When $2$ is invertible, an involutive $\alpha$ splits $A = A_+ \oplus A_-$, where $A_\pm = \{x \in A : \alpha(x) = \pm x\}$.
Indeed, for $x \in A$,
\[
x = \tfrac12\bigl(x + \alpha(x)\bigr) + \tfrac12\bigl(x - \alpha(x)\bigr).
\]
We call the elements of $A_+$ \emph{symmetric} and the elements of $A_-$ \emph{skew}.

Let $\alpha$ be involutive, of either kind.
Then $\sigma_\alpha(x \otimes y) = \alpha(y) \otimes \alpha(x)$ is a $K$-linear involution of $A \otimes A$.
It is the composite of the swap and $\alpha \otimes \alpha$, as the following diagram shows.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\tau"] \arrow[rr, bend left=40, "\sigma_\alpha"] & A \otimes A \arrow[r, "\alpha \otimes \alpha"] & A \otimes A
\end{tikzcd}
\]
So $\sigma_\alpha$ defines a symmetrized product as in Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}.
The product it defines is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twisted-product}
x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(y)\alpha(x).
\end{equation}
For $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$ the product $\mu_{\sigma_\alpha}$ is the Jordan product.
If $\alpha$ is an automorphism, then $\alpha(y)\alpha(x) = \alpha(yx)$.
If $\alpha$ is an anti-automorphism, then $\alpha(y)\alpha(x) = \alpha(xy)$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:defect}
The \emph{Leibniz defect} of a linear map $D \colon A \to A$ is the bilinear map $E_D \colon A \times A \to A$ given, for $x, y \in A$, by
\[
E_D(x,y) = D(xy) - D(x)y - xD(y).
\]
\end{definition}

The name is ours, and the map is classical: \citet[\S2]{Cusack1975} writes it $d(a,b) = D(ab) - aDb - (Da)b$ and studies Jordan derivations through it.
We recall the Hochschild complex of $A$ with coefficients in $A$ \citep[\S9.1]{Weibel1994}.
An \emph{$n$-cochain} is a $K$-multilinear map from $A^n$ to $A$.
So a $0$-cochain is an element of $A$, and a $1$-cochain is a linear map $D \colon A \to A$.
The \emph{coboundary} $\delta$ sends each $n$-cochain to an $(n+1)$-cochain, and $\delta \circ \delta = 0$ \citep[\S9.1]{Weibel1994}.
An \emph{$n$-cocycle} is an $n$-cochain $f$ with $\delta f = 0$.
The $n$-th \emph{Hochschild cohomology} $HH^n(A, A)$ is the space of $n$-cocycles modulo the images $\delta g$ of the $(n-1)$-cochains $g$ \citep[\S9.1]{Weibel1994}.

On a $1$-cochain $D$ the coboundary is $(\delta D)(x,y) = xD(y) - D(xy) + D(x)y$ \citep[\S2, Definition 2.1]{Hochschild1945}.
So $E_D = -\delta D$.
Thus $D$ is a derivation exactly when $E_D = 0$, so derivations are the $1$-cocycles.
Since $\delta \circ \delta = 0$, the defect is a $2$-cocycle: for all $a, b, c \in A$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cocycle}
aE_D(b,c) - E_D(ab,c) + E_D(a,bc) - E_D(a,b)c = 0.
\end{equation}
This says that $E_D$ is a cocycle of the Hochschild complex \citep[\S9.1]{Weibel1994}.

The defect also describes the other classes of Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}.
Expanding $D(x \circ y)$ shows that $D$ is a Jordan derivation exactly when $E_D(x,y) + E_D(y,x) = 0$ for all $x, y$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:defect}
Let $A$ be a unital associative $K$-algebra, let $\alpha$ be an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism of $A$, and let $D \colon A \to A$ be a linear map with $D\alpha = \alpha D$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $\alpha$ is an automorphism, then $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ if and only if $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(y,x)) = 0$ for all $x, y \in A$.
\item If $\alpha$ is an anti-automorphism, then $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ if and only if $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(x,y)) = 0$ for all $x, y \in A$.
\end{enumerate}
In both cases every derivation that commutes with $\alpha$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $E = E_D$.
By Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}, $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ means $D(x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y) = D(x) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y + x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} D(y)$ for all $x, y$.
We compute the difference of the two sides in each case.

For (i), the product~\eqref{eq:twisted-product} is $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(yx)$.
Since $D\alpha = \alpha D$, the left side is $D(xy) + \alpha(D(yx))$.
The right side is $D(x)y + \alpha(yD(x)) + xD(y) + \alpha(D(y)x)$.
We group the terms without $\alpha$ and the terms with $\alpha$.
The first group gives $D(xy) - D(x)y - xD(y) = E(x,y)$.
Since $\alpha$ is linear, the second group gives $\alpha\bigl(D(yx) - D(y)x - yD(x)\bigr) = \alpha(E(y,x))$.
So the left side minus the right side is $E(x,y) + \alpha(E(y,x))$, which proves (i).

The proof of (ii) follows the same steps as the proof of (i).
Here the product~\eqref{eq:twisted-product} is $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(xy)$.
Since $D\alpha = \alpha D$, the left side is
\[
D(xy) + \alpha(D(xy)),
\]
and the right side is
\[
D(x)y + \alpha(D(x)y) + xD(y) + \alpha(xD(y)).
\]
The same grouping gives the difference
\[
E(x,y) + \alpha(E(x,y)),
\]
which proves (ii).

If $D$ is a derivation, then $E = 0$, and both conditions hold.
\end{proof}

Without the hypothesis $D\alpha = \alpha D$ the same computation gives the residual identities
\[
D(x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y) - D(x) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y - x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} D(y)
= E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(y,x)) + (D\alpha - \alpha D)(yx)
\]
in case (i), and the same with $\alpha(E_D(x,y))$ and $(D\alpha - \alpha D)(xy)$ in case (ii).
So the hypothesis $D\alpha = \alpha D$ removes exactly the last term, and Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} says that the remaining two terms vanish.

The computation uses neither the unitality of $A$ nor the involutivity of $\alpha$; involutivity is needed only for $\sigma_\alpha$ to be an involution.

We now read the two conditions; the second needs no hypothesis, and for the first we assume that $2$ is invertible.

In case (ii), the equation $E_D + \alpha(E_D) = 0$ says $\alpha(E_D) = -E_D$.
So $E_D$ takes values in $A_-$, the skew elements of $\alpha$.
In case (i), we write $E_D = E_{\mathrm{s}} + E_{\mathrm{a}}$.
Here $E_{\mathrm{s}}(x,y) = \tfrac12(E_D(x,y) + E_D(y,x))$ is symmetric.
And $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) = \tfrac12(E_D(x,y) - E_D(y,x))$ is antisymmetric.

We swap $x$ and $y$ in the condition of (i).
Then we take the sum and the difference of the two equations.
So the condition holds exactly when $E_{\mathrm{s}}$ takes values in $A_-$ and $E_{\mathrm{a}}$ takes values in $A_+$.
Now let $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$.
Then $A_- = 0$ and $A_+ = A$, so the condition says $E_{\mathrm{s}} = 0$.
This is the Jordan condition, so Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i) recovers the swap case of Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:inner-twisted}
Let $\alpha$ be an involutive automorphism or anti-automorphism of $A$, and let $D \in \Der(A)$.
Then $E_D = 0$.
By~\eqref{eq:twisted-product}, the two sides of the $\sigma_\alpha$-identity differ by
\[
D(\alpha(yx)) - \alpha(D(yx)),
\]
and for an anti-automorphism the same computation with $\alpha(xy)$ in place of $\alpha(yx)$ applies.
Since $A$ is unital, the products $yx$ span $A$.
So $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ if and only if $D\alpha = \alpha D$.

Next we look at the inner derivations $\operatorname{ad}_g$ in this class.
The condition $\operatorname{ad}_g \alpha = \alpha \operatorname{ad}_g$ means
\[
[g - \alpha(g), \alpha(x)] = 0 \qquad \text{for all } x.
\]
Since $\alpha$ is bijective, this holds if and only if $g - \alpha(g) \in Z(A)$.
If $\alpha$ is an anti-automorphism instead, the same computation with $\alpha(x)\alpha(g)$ in place of $\alpha(xg)$ gives $[g + \alpha(g), \alpha(x)] = 0$ for all $x$, that is, $g + \alpha(g) \in Z(A)$.
In the rest of this remark $\alpha$ is an automorphism.
Let $\Inn_\alpha(A)$ be the space of the maps $\operatorname{ad}_g$ with $g - \alpha(g) \in Z(A)$.
These are the inner derivations in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$, so $\Inn_\alpha(A) = \Inn(A) \cap \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
The quotient $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha / \Inn_\alpha(A)$ is the space of \emph{twisted outer derivations}.
\end{remark}

\citet[Corollary 5]{Cusack1975} and \citet[Theorem 1]{Bresar1988} proved that a Jordan derivation of a $2$-torsion-free semiprime ring is a derivation.

\begin{theorem}[{\citep[Theorem 1]{Bresar1988}}]\label{thm:bresar}
Let $R$ be a $2$-torsion-free semiprime ring. Then every Jordan derivation $D \colon R \to R$ is a derivation.
\end{theorem}

The next corollary applies Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar} to the twisted derivations.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:twisted-semiprime}
Let $A$ be a $2$-torsion-free semiprime $K$-algebra with an involutive $K$-algebra automorphism or anti-automorphism $\alpha$.
Then $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha \cap \JDer(A) = \Der(A)^\alpha$, where $\Der(A)^\alpha$ is the set of derivations commuting with $\alpha$ (not to be confused with the space $\ADer(A)$ of $\alpha$-derivations of Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Every derivation is a Jordan derivation by Remark~\ref{rem:sigma-derivation-classification}.
With Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}, this gives $\Der(A)^\alpha \subseteq \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha \cap \JDer(A)$.
Conversely, every Jordan derivation of $A$ is a derivation by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}, which applies since a $K$-linear map is additive; the $\circ$ form of the Jordan condition and Herstein's $x^2$ form agree by the linearization of Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}, which uses the $2$-torsion-freeness assumed here.
So a map in the intersection is a derivation that commutes with $\alpha$.
\end{proof}

\section{Reduced commutative algebras and twisted derivations}\label{sec:twisted-commutative}

For commutative algebras the Jordan hypothesis in Corollary~\ref{cor:twisted-semiprime} can be dropped.
Recall from Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations} that a reduced commutative algebra has no nonzero nilpotent element.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:twisted-commutative}
Let $A$ be a commutative reduced $K$-algebra with $2$ invertible in $K$, and let $\alpha$ be an involutive automorphism of $A$.
Then $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha = \Der(A)^\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every derivation commuting with $\alpha$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}.
Conversely, let $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
Write $E = E_D$.
Since $A$ is commutative, $E$ is symmetric.
So $E$ takes values in $A_-$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i).

Since $D$ commutes with $\alpha$, it maps each of $A_+$ and $A_-$ into itself.
So if $x$ and $y$ lie in the same part, then $E(x,y)$ lies in
\[
A_+ \cap A_- = 0.
\]

Now let $x \in A_+$ and $y \in A_-$, and put $z = E(x,y)$.
Then $z$ lies in $A_-$, and so do $xy$, $zx$ and $xz$, while $zy$ lies in $A_+$.
Apply the cocycle identity~\eqref{eq:cocycle} to $(a,b,c) = (z,x,y)$:
\[
zE(x,y) - E(zx,y) + E(z,xy) - E(z,x)y = 0.
\]
The pairs $(zx,y)$ and $(z,xy)$ lie in $A_-$, so $E$ vanishes on them, and we get $z^2 = E(z,x)y$.
Apply~\eqref{eq:cocycle} to $(a,b,c) = (x,z,y)$:
\[
xE(z,y) - E(xz,y) + E(x,zy) - E(x,z)y = 0.
\]
The pairs $(z,y)$ and $(xz,y)$ lie in $A_-$, and the pair $(x,zy)$ lies in $A_+$, so $E$ vanishes on all three and $E(x,z)y = 0$.
Since $E$ is symmetric, $z^2 = E(z,x)y = E(x,z)y = 0$.
So $z = 0$ because $A$ is reduced.
Since $E$ is bilinear and symmetric and $A = A_+ \oplus A_-$, we get $E = 0$.
Hence $D$ is a derivation.
The proof uses the commutativity of $A$ only to make $E_D$ symmetric, and reducedness only to pass from $z \in A_-$ and $z^2 = 0$ to $z = 0$.
It therefore gives two stronger statements.
First, ``reduced'' may be replaced by ``no nonzero $z \in A_-$ satisfies $z^2 = 0$'', a condition that is empty when $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$.
Second, commutativity is used nowhere else in the argument: for any unital $K$-algebra $A$ with $2$ invertible, if $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ has symmetric defect $E_D$ and no nonzero $z \in A_-$ satisfies $z^2 = 0$, then $D$ is a derivation.
Remark~\ref{rem:commutativity-needed} shows that commutativity cannot be dropped from the theorem itself.
\end{proof}

Example~\ref{ex:twisted-nilpotent} shows that the reduced hypothesis cannot be dropped, and Example~\ref{ex:twisted-char-two} shows the same for the invertibility of $2$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:twisted-nilpotent}
Let $A = \mathbb{F}_5[u,v]/(u^2, v^2)$.
It has basis $\{1, u, v, uv\}$.
Let $\alpha$ fix $u$ and send $v$ to $-v$.
Let $D$ send $uv$ to $uv$ and the other three basis elements to $0$.
Then $D$ commutes with $\alpha$.
It satisfies the Leibniz rule for $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y$ on all basis pairs.
So $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
But $D$ is not a derivation, since
\[
D(uv) = uv \qquad \text{while} \qquad D(u)v + uD(v) = 0.
\]
A direct computation verifies the three statements: commutation with $\alpha$ is linear in $D$ and is checked on a basis, and the Leibniz rule is linear in $D$ and bilinear in the pair, so it too is checked on basis pairs; the failure of the derivation rule is read at the single pair $(u,v)$.
Here $z = E(u,v) = uv$ is a nonzero element with $z^2 = 0$.
\end{example}

The next example shows that the invertibility of $2$ is needed in Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative}.

\begin{example}\label{ex:twisted-char-two}
Let $A = \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_2$, which is commutative and reduced, and let $\alpha$ swap the two factors.
Put $p = (1,0)$, $q = (0,1)$ and $e = (1,1)$, and let $D$ be the linear map with $D(p) = D(q) = e$, so that $D(e) = 0$.
Then $D$ commutes with $\alpha$.
On the basis pairs $(p,p)$, $(q,q)$ and $(p,q)$, both sides of the Leibniz rule for $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + \alpha(xy)$ are zero.
Here $p \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} p = e$, so the two sides vanish because $D(e) = 0$ and $2e = 0$, not because the products vanish.
For a pair with $e$ in one entry, $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} e = x + \alpha(x)$ lies in $\{0, e\}$, so the left side vanishes because $D(e) = D(0) = 0$, and the right side is $D(x) \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} e$, which vanishes because $D(x)$ lies in $\{0, e\}$ and $e \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} e = 2e = 0$.
So $D \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
But $D$ is not a derivation: since $p^2 = p$, a derivation would satisfy $D(p) = D(p^2) = 2pD(p) = 0$, while $D(p) = e$.
\end{example}

Our second natural candidate for the twisted derivations is the space $\ADer(A)$ of $\alpha$-derivations.
It was defined for automorphisms in Section~\ref{sec:sigma-derivations}.
It differs from $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ in both directions, and one algebra shows both failures.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twisted}
Let $A = \mathbb{F}_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^3)$, and let $\alpha$ be the automorphism with $\alpha(\varepsilon) = -\varepsilon$.
Then
\[
\ADer(A) \not\subseteq \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha \not\subseteq \ADer(A).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The algebra $A$ has basis $\{1, \varepsilon, \varepsilon^2\}$.
The map $\alpha$ fixes $1$ and $\varepsilon^2$ and sends $\varepsilon$ to $-\varepsilon$.
So it is an involutive automorphism with $A_+ = \mathbb{F}_3 1 + \mathbb{F}_3\varepsilon^2$ and $A_- = \mathbb{F}_3\varepsilon$.
Since $A$ is commutative, $E_D$ is symmetric for every linear map $D$.
Let $D$ be a linear map with $D\alpha = \alpha D$.
Since $E_D$ is symmetric, the condition $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(y,x)) = 0$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}(i) reads $E_D(x,y) + \alpha(E_D(x,y)) = 0$, that is, $E_D$ takes values in $A_-$; so $D$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ exactly when this holds.
We give one map for each non-inclusion.
Both maps are diagonal on the basis, so both commute with $\alpha$.

For the first non-inclusion, let $D(a + b\varepsilon + c\varepsilon^2) = b\varepsilon + c\varepsilon^2$.
We check that $D$ is an $\alpha$-derivation on pairs of basis elements.
Pairs that contain $1$ give $D$ of the other element of the pair on both sides, since $D(1) = 0$ and $\alpha(1) = 1$.
For the pair $(\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, the left side is $D(\varepsilon^2) = \varepsilon^2$.
The right side is $D(\varepsilon)\alpha(\varepsilon) + \alpha(\varepsilon)D(\varepsilon) = -2\varepsilon^2 = \varepsilon^2$.
The remaining pairs have product $0$, and each term on the right side is a multiple of $\varepsilon^3 = 0$.
So $D \in \ADer(A)$.
On the other hand, $E_D(\varepsilon, \varepsilon) = D(\varepsilon^2) - 2\varepsilon D(\varepsilon) = \varepsilon^2 - 2\varepsilon^2 = 2\varepsilon^2$.
This element is nonzero and lies in $A_+$, so it does not lie in $A_-$.
Hence $D \notin \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.

The second non-inclusion uses a derivation.

Let $d$ be the linear map
\[
d(a + b\varepsilon + c\varepsilon^2) = b\varepsilon + 2c\varepsilon^2.
\]
It is a derivation, since $d(\varepsilon^i) = i\varepsilon^i$ and exponents add under multiplication.
So $E_d = 0$, and $d \in \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
On the other hand,
\[
d(\varepsilon^2) - d(\varepsilon)\alpha(\varepsilon) - \alpha(\varepsilon)d(\varepsilon) = 2\varepsilon^2 + 2\varepsilon^2 = \varepsilon^2 \neq 0.
\]
Hence $d \notin \ADer(A)$.
\end{proof}

The two classes can also meet, even when one of them is not defined.

\begin{example}\label{ex:twisted-meet}
Let $A = M_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$ and let $\alpha$ be the transpose, an involutive anti-automorphism.
Then $\ADer(A)$ is not defined, since it needs an automorphism, so this part gives a twisted derivation with no $\alpha$-derivation to compare with.
Take $g = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is nonzero and skew for the transpose, and let $\operatorname{ad}_g$ be the inner derivation $x \mapsto [g,x]$.
It commutes with $\alpha$ by the anti-automorphism case of Remark~\ref{rem:inner-twisted}, since $g + \alpha(g) = 0$, and it has $E = 0$, so it lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}.

Now let $A = K[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ with $\alpha(\varepsilon) = -\varepsilon$.
The map $D(a + b\varepsilon) = b\varepsilon$ is a derivation and an $\alpha$-derivation, and it commutes with $\alpha$.
So it lies in both $\ADer(A)$ and $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:defect}.
A direct computation gives that when $\operatorname{char} K \neq 2$ both classes are one-dimensional, spanned by $\varepsilon \mapsto \varepsilon$, so here they coincide; when $\operatorname{char} K = 2$ the map $\alpha$ is the identity, $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)$ is the whole of $\mathrm{End}_K(A)$, and $\ADer(A) = \Der(A)$ is two-dimensional.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Reducedness and commutativity cannot both be dropped]\label{rem:commutativity-needed}
Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} fails when the hypotheses that $A$ is commutative and that $A$ is reduced are both dropped.
Let $A$ be the four-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_3$-algebra with basis $1, t, v, w$, in which $1$ is a unit and
\[
t^2 = 0, \qquad tv = vt = tw = wt = 0, \qquad v^2 = w^2 = wv = t, \qquad vw = 0,
\]
and let $\alpha$ be the linear map with $\alpha(t) = t$, $\alpha(v) = -v$ and $\alpha(w) = -w$.
Then $A$ is associative, a check on the $64$ products of basis elements, and $\alpha$ is an involutive automorphism.
The linear map $D$ with $D(v) = v + w$ and $D(1) = D(t) = D(w) = 0$ commutes with $\alpha$ and satisfies the Leibniz rule for $\cdot_{\sigma_\alpha}$ on every pair of basis elements; since that rule is linear in $D$ and bilinear in the pair, $D$ lies in $\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha$.
It is not a derivation, since $D(vw) = 0$ while $D(v)w + vD(w) = vw + w^2 = t \neq 0$.
Solving the linear conditions on the four basis elements gives $\dim \BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(A)^\alpha = 3$ and $\dim \Der(A)^\alpha = 2$.
The defect is antisymmetric at $(v,w)$, where $E_D(v,w) = -t$, and $A_-$ does contain a nonzero square-zero element, $(v+w)^2 = 0$; this is consistent with the symmetric-defect statement at the end of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative}.
The algebra is neither commutative ($vw = 0$ while $wv = t$) nor reduced ($t \ne 0$ and $t^2 = 0$), so the example does not show that commutativity alone is needed, and we make no such claim.
A witness that drops commutativity while keeping reducedness is not to be had in finite dimension over a finite field: the radical of a finite-dimensional algebra is nilpotent \citep[\S4.4]{Pierce1982}, hence zero in a reduced algebra, so the algebra is semisimple; by Wedderburn's structure theorem it is then a product of matrix algebras over division rings \citep[\S3.5]{Pierce1982}, and reducedness forces the matrix sizes to be $1$, since $e_{12}^2 = 0$ in $M_n(D)$ for $n \geq 2$; so it is a product of division rings, each of which is a field when the base field is finite.
The first candidate is therefore a non-commutative division algebra, such as the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$; there $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$ gives $\sigma_\alpha = \tau$, so the assertion becomes $\JDer(\mathbb{H}) = \Der(\mathbb{H})$, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}.
The quaternions do not provide a witness either.
Every automorphism of $\mathbb{H}$ is inner \citep[\S12.6]{Pierce1982}, so the involutive ones are the identity and the conjugations by pure imaginary quaternions, two of the latter are conjugate, since $u q u^{-1} = q'$ for $u = 1 - q'q$ when $q, q'$ are pure imaginary of norm $1$ with $q' \ne -q$, while $q' = -q$ gives the same conjugation; the structure constants are rational, so one computation settles all of them, and solving the linear conditions on the sixteen coordinates of $D$ gives $\dim\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(\mathbb{H})^\alpha = \dim\Der(\mathbb{H})^\alpha = 1$.
So the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} holds at $\mathbb{H}$ for every involutive $\alpha$, although $\mathbb{H}$ is not commutative.
The same computation gives $\dim\BSDer_{\sigma_\alpha}(\mathbb{H}) = 1 < 3 = \dim\Der(\mathbb{H})$: at $\mathbb{H}$ the derivations lie in the twisted class only in so far as they commute with $\alpha$.
For a general reduced algebra the argument of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} reduces the question to the skew part.
Let $E = E_D$ and $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) = E(x,y) - E(y,x)$.
Then $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) = D([x,y]) - [D(x),y] - [x,D(y)]$, so $E_{\mathrm{a}} = 0$ says that $D$ is a derivation for the commutator bracket, and $E_{\mathrm{a}}$ takes values in $A_+$.
Since $\alpha$ is multiplicative and $D$ commutes with it, $E$ maps $A_i \times A_j$ into $A_{i+j}$; since $E$ is additive in each argument and $A = A_+ + A_-$, three cases remain.
If $x \in A_+$ and $y \in A_-$ then $E(x,y)$ and $E(y,x)$ lie in $A_-$, so $E_{\mathrm{a}}(x,y) \in A_+ \cap A_- = 0$.
If $x, y \in A_+$ then $x \cdot_{\sigma_\alpha} y = xy + yx$, so the restriction of $D$ to $A_+$ is a Jordan derivation of $A_+$, a reduced, hence semiprime, and $2$-torsion-free ring, and is therefore a derivation there by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}; hence $E(x,y) = 0$.
So only $x, y \in A_-$ remains, where $E$ is alternating with values in $A_+$.
If it vanishes there then $E_{\mathrm{a}} = 0$, the defect is symmetric, and the argument at the end of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted-commutative} makes $D$ a derivation; whether it must vanish is what is missing, and it is not settled here.
The answer is nevertheless positive in four classes of algebras, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:positive}: if $A$ is a quaternion division algebra over $K$ and $\alpha$ is any involutive automorphism; if $A$ is reduced and its centre contains a unit $z$ with $\alpha(z) = -z$; if $A_+$ is a commutative domain and $A_- = A_+Y$ for an element $Y$ that is not a zero divisor; and if $A = \prod_{i \in I} B_i$ is reduced and $\alpha$ permutes the factors by an involution of $I$ without fixed points.
\end{remark}

\appendix

\section{The four positive cases of Remark~\ref{rem:commutativity-needed}}\label{app:positive}

Throughout, $A$ is a unital $K$-algebra with $2$ invertible, $\alpha$ is an involutive automorphism, $D \colon A \to A$ is $K$-linear with $D\alpha = \alpha D$ and satisfies the twisted Leibniz rule for $\sigma_\alpha$, and $E = E_D$ is its defect.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:defect} this is equivalent to $E(x,y) + \alpha(E(y,x)) = 0$ for all $x, y$.
Since $\alpha$ is multiplicative and $D$ commutes with it, $E$ maps $A_i \times A_j$ into $A_{i+j}$, so that relation splits into the three parity cases
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-parity}
\begin{aligned}
&E(x,y) = -E(y,x) && (x, y \in A_+),\\
&E(x,y) = E(y,x) && (x \in A_+, \ y \in A_-),\\
&E(x,y) = -E(y,x) && (x, y \in A_-),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and by the reduction in Remark~\ref{rem:commutativity-needed} the restriction of $D$ to $A_+$ is a Jordan derivation of $A_+$, hence a derivation of $A_+$ when $A_+$ is reduced and $2$-torsion-free, by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}.

\subsection{Quaternion division algebras}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:app-quaternion}
Let $A$ be a quaternion division algebra over $K$ and let $\alpha$ be an involutive $K$-automorphism of $A$. Then $D$ is a derivation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\alpha = \mathrm{id}$, then $\sigma_\alpha = \tau$ and $D$ is a Jordan derivation, and $A$ is a division ring, hence $2$-torsion-free and semiprime, so $D$ is a derivation by Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}.

Let $\alpha \neq \mathrm{id}$. A quaternion algebra is central simple over $K$, so every $K$-automorphism of $A$ is inner \citep[\S12.6]{Pierce1982}: there is a unit $u$ with $\alpha(x) = uxu^{-1}$. From $\alpha^2 = \mathrm{id}$ and $Z(A) = K$ we get $d := u^2 \in K^\times$, and $u \notin K$. Put $L := K(u)$; then $L = A_+$, and $L = K(\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon^2 = d$. Fix $0 \neq j \in A_-$. Then $j \notin L$, $A = L \oplus Lj$, $j^2 =: c \in K^\times$, and $j\ell = \sigma(\ell)j$ for $\ell \in L$, where $\sigma$ is the nontrivial $K$-automorphism of $L$: indeed $\ell \mapsto j\ell j^{-1}$ is a $K$-automorphism of the field $L$, and it is not the identity because $j \notin L$.

By \eqref{eq:app-parity} and Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}, the restriction of $D$ to $L$ is a derivation of $L$, and $0 = \delta(\varepsilon^2) = 2\varepsilon\delta(\varepsilon)$ gives $\delta = 0$. Write $D(mj) = \eta(m)j$ for $m \in L$, with $\eta \colon L \to L$ additive. For $\ell, m, n \in L$ the second and third cases of \eqref{eq:app-parity} read
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-quat}
\begin{aligned}
\eta(\ell m) - \ell\eta(m) &= \eta(m\sigma(\ell)) - \eta(m)\sigma(\ell),\\
\eta(m)\sigma(n) + m\sigma(\eta(n)) + \eta(n)\sigma(m) + n\sigma(\eta(m)) &= 0,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
the first from $\ell\,mj = \ell m\,j$ and $mj\,\ell = m\sigma(\ell)j$ after cancelling $j$, the second from $(mj)(nj) = c\,m\sigma(n)$ and $D|_L = 0$ after cancelling the unit $c$.
In \eqref{eq:app-quat}, $m = n = 1$ gives $\eta(1) + \sigma(\eta(1)) = 0$, so $\eta(1) = \mu\varepsilon$ with $\mu \in K$; $m = n = \varepsilon$ gives $\eta(\varepsilon)\sigma(\varepsilon) + \varepsilon\sigma(\eta(\varepsilon)) = 0$, which for $\eta(\varepsilon) = \nu_0 + \nu_1\varepsilon$ is $-2\nu_1d = 0$, so $\eta(\varepsilon) = \nu_0 \in K$; and the first identity with $\ell = \varepsilon$ and $m = 1$ gives $2\nu_0 = \varepsilon\eta(1) - \eta(1)\sigma(\varepsilon) = 2\mu d$, so $\nu_0 = \mu d$. Hence $\eta(x_0 + x_1\varepsilon) = \mu(x_0\varepsilon + x_1 d) = \mu\varepsilon x$ for all $x \in L$.
So $D$ vanishes on $L$, and $D(mj) = \mu\varepsilon m\,j = \operatorname{ad}_{\mu\varepsilon/2}(mj)$ because $j(\mu\varepsilon/2) = \sigma(\mu\varepsilon/2)j = -(\mu\varepsilon/2)j$. Thus $D = \operatorname{ad}_{\mu\varepsilon/2}$ is a derivation.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A central odd unit}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:app-lambda}
Let $B$ be a ring in which $2$ is invertible and let $\lambda \colon B \to B$ be additive with $\lambda([a,b]) = [a,\lambda(b)]$ and $\lambda(a) \circ b + a \circ \lambda(b) = 0$ for all $a, b \in B$. Then $\lambda = 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $c = \lambda(1)$. The second identity with $b = 1$ gives $2\lambda(a) + a \circ c = 0$, hence $\lambda(a) = -\tfrac12(a \circ c)$. Substituting this form back into the second identity and multiplying by $2$ gives $(a \circ c) \circ b + a \circ (b \circ c) = 0$ for all $a, b$; with $b = 1$ this reads $4(a \circ c) = 0$, so $a \circ c = 0$ and $\lambda(a) = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:app-central-odd}
Let $A$ be reduced and suppose that $Z(A)$ contains a unit $z$ with $\alpha(z) = -z$. Then $D$ is a derivation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $c = z^2$. Then $c$ is central, $\alpha(c) = c$, and $c$ is a unit. Every $a \in A$ is $a_+ + a_-$ with $a_\pm \in A_\pm$, and $a_- = z(z^{-1}a_-) \in zA_+$, so $A = A_+ \oplus zA_+$ and $A \cong A_+[z]/(z^2-c)$ as $A_+$-algebras.
The restriction $\delta := D|_{A_+}$ is a derivation of $A_+$ by \eqref{eq:app-parity} and Theorem~\ref{thm:bresar}, and $\delta(c)$ is central in $A_+$: $c$ is central, so $0 = \delta([a,c]) = [\delta(a),c] + [a,\delta(c)] = [a,\delta(c)]$.
Write $D(mz) = \eta(m)z$ for $m \in A_+$, with $\eta \colon A_+ \to A_+$ additive. The second and third cases of \eqref{eq:app-parity} read
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-central}
\eta([e,m]) = [\delta(e),m] + [e,\eta(m)], \qquad
\delta(c)\,(m \circ n) + c\,\delta(m \circ n) = c\bigl(\eta(m) \circ n + m \circ \eta(n)\bigr)
\end{equation}
for $e, m, n \in A_+$: the first from $E(e,mz) = E(mz,e)$, using $(em)z = e\,mz$ and $mz\,e = (me)z$, the second from $E(mz,nz) + E(nz,mz) = 0$, using $(mz)(nz) = c\,mn$ and $D(cm'n') = \delta(c)m'n' + c\,\delta(m'n')$, which holds because $E$ vanishes on $A_+ \times A_+$.
Let $\tilde\eta := \delta + \frac{\delta(c)}{2c}$, with $\frac{\delta(c)}{2c}$ central in $A_+$. It satisfies both identities of \eqref{eq:app-central}: for the first,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde\eta([e,m]) &= \delta([e,m]) + \tfrac{\delta(c)}{2c}[e,m]\\
&= [\delta(e),m] + [e,\delta(m)] + \bigl[\tfrac{\delta(c)}{2c}e,\,m\bigr]
= [\delta(e),m] + [e,\tilde\eta(m)],
\end{aligned}
\]
and for the second, $\delta(c)(m \circ n) + c\,\delta(m \circ n) = \delta(c)(m \circ n) + c(\delta(m) \circ n + m \circ \delta(n)) = c(\tilde\eta(m) \circ n + m \circ \tilde\eta(n))$.
So $\lambda := \eta - \tilde\eta$ satisfies Lemma~\ref{lem:app-lambda} with $B = A_+$, and $\lambda = 0$.
Hence $D(a + mz) = \delta(a) + \delta(m)z + m\frac{\delta(c)}{2c}z$ for $a, m \in A_+$, which is the derivation of $A_+[z]/(z^2-c)$ that extends $\delta$ and sends $z$ to $\frac{\delta(c)}{2c}z$; the relation $z^2 = c$ is respected because $2z\cdot\frac{\delta(c)}{2c}z = \delta(c)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The odd part of rank one over a commutative domain}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:app-rank-one}
Suppose that $A_+ =: R$ is a commutative domain, that $A_- = RY$ for an element $Y$ that is not a zero divisor, and that $Y^2 =: c$. Then $D$ is a derivation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $Y$ is not a zero divisor, each $a \in R$ has a unique $\sigma(a) \in R$ with $Ya = \sigma(a)Y$, and $\sigma$ is an automorphism of $R$. For $r, r', b, b' \in R$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-rank-mult}
(r + bY)(r' + b'Y) = rr' + c\,b\sigma(b') + (rb' + b\sigma(r'))Y,
\end{equation}
and comparing $(bY \cdot b'Y)\cdot b''Y$ with $bY \cdot (b'Y \cdot b''Y)$, which gives $c\,b\sigma(b')b'' = b\sigma(c)\sigma(b')\sigma^2(b'')$, we get, with $b = 1$ and then $b'' = 1$ and since $c$ and $\sigma(b')$ are not zero divisors,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-rank-sigma}
\sigma(c) = c, \qquad \sigma^2 = \mathrm{id}.
\end{equation}
Write $p := D|_R$ and $D(bY) = \psi(b)Y$ with $\psi \colon R \to R$ additive. On $R \times R$ the twisted product is $2rr'$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-rank-p}
p(rr') = p(r)r' + rp(r'), \qquad p(1) = 0 .
\end{equation}
The second and third cases of \eqref{eq:app-parity} read
\begin{align}
\psi(rb - b\sigma(r)) &= p(r)b - b\sigma(p(r)) + r\psi(b) - \psi(b)\sigma(r), \label{eq:app-rank-M}\\
p\bigl(c[b\sigma(b') + b'\sigma(b)]\bigr) &= c\bigl[\psi(b)\sigma(b') + b'\sigma(\psi(b)) + b\sigma(\psi(b')) + \psi(b')\sigma(b)\bigr] \label{eq:app-rank-O}
\end{align}
for $r, b, b' \in R$: the first from $E(r,bY) = E(bY,r)$, using $r\,bY = rbY$ and $bY\,r = b\sigma(r)Y$, the second from $E(bY,b'Y) + E(b'Y,bY) = 0$, using $(bY)(b'Y) = c\,b\sigma(b')$.
Put $\delta := \psi - p$, $w := \delta(1)$, $\theta := p\sigma - \sigma p$ and $T := \delta - (\cdot)w$. Substituting $\psi = p + \delta$ into \eqref{eq:app-rank-M} and \eqref{eq:app-rank-O}, using \eqref{eq:app-rank-p} and cancelling, gives
\begin{align}
\delta(rb) - r\delta(b) &= \delta(b\sigma(r)) - \delta(b)\sigma(r) + b\theta(r), \label{eq:app-rank-7}\\
p(c)[b\sigma(b') + b'\sigma(b)] + c[b\theta(b') + b'\theta(b)] \notag\\
 &= c\bigl[\delta(b)\sigma(b') + b'\sigma(\delta(b)) \notag\\
 &\qquad + b\sigma(\delta(b')) + \delta(b')\sigma(b)\bigr], \label{eq:app-rank-8}
\end{align}
and \eqref{eq:app-rank-O} with $b = b' = 1$ gives $p(c) = c(w + \sigma(w))$. Replacing $\delta$ by $T + (\cdot)w$ in \eqref{eq:app-rank-7} and \eqref{eq:app-rank-8} and using \eqref{eq:app-rank-sigma} and the commutativity of $R$, the terms containing $w$ alone cancel, and dividing \eqref{eq:app-rank-8} by the non-zero-divisor $c$ leaves
\begin{align}
T(rb) - rT(b) &= T(b\sigma(r)) - T(b)\sigma(r) + b\eta(r), \qquad \eta(r) := \theta(r) - w\sigma(r) + \sigma(r)w, \label{eq:app-rank-7p}\\
b\theta(b') + b'\theta(b) &= T(b)\sigma(b') + b'\sigma(T(b)) + b\sigma(T(b')) + T(b')\sigma(b), \label{eq:app-rank-8p}
\end{align}
where \eqref{eq:app-rank-7p} at $b = 1$ gives $\eta(r) = T(r) - T(\sigma(r))$ and \eqref{eq:app-rank-8p} at $b' = 1$ gives $\theta(b) = T(b) + \sigma(T(b))$. Substituting the latter into \eqref{eq:app-rank-8p} and using $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{id}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-rank-11}
T(b)\bigl(\sigma(b') - b'\bigr) + T(b')\bigl(\sigma(b) - b\bigr) = 0 \qquad (b, b' \in R).
\end{equation}
If $\sigma \neq \mathrm{id}$, choose $u \in R$ with $u_- := \tfrac12(u - \sigma(u)) \neq 0$. Then \eqref{eq:app-rank-11} says $T(b')(-2b'_-) = -T(b)(-2b_-)$, so $T(b')b_- = T(b)b'_-$ for all $b, b'$; fixing $b$ with $b_- \neq 0$, there is $\mu$ in the fraction field $F$ of $R$ with $T(b) = \mu b_-$ for all $b \in R$. Substituting this into \eqref{eq:app-rank-7p}, with $r = r_+ + r_-$, $b = b_+ + b_-$ and $\eta(r) = T(r) - T(\sigma(r)) = 2\mu r_-$, gives
\[
\mu(r_-b_+ - r_-b_-) = \mu(b_+r_- + 3b_-r_-),
\]
that is, $-4\mu b_-r_- = 0$, and taking $b = r$ with $r_- \neq 0$ gives $\mu = 0$, since $F$ is a field of characteristic different from $2$. If $\sigma = \mathrm{id}$, then \eqref{eq:app-rank-8p} at $b' = 1$ and \eqref{eq:app-rank-7p} give $2bT(r) = 0$, so again $T = 0$.
So $\delta(b) = bw$ for all $b \in R$, and $p(c) = c(w + \sigma(w))$. By \eqref{eq:app-rank-mult}, $D(r + bY) = D(r) + D(b)Y + b\,D(Y)$ with $D(Y) = wY$ on the generators $R$ and $Y$ of $A$, and the relation $Y^2 = c$ is respected, so $D$ is the derivation of $A$ determined by $p$ on $R$ and $Y \mapsto wY$. Explicitly, $E(r,r') = 0$ by \eqref{eq:app-rank-p}, $E(r,bY) = [\delta(rb) - r\delta(b)]Y = 0$ because $\delta = (\cdot)w$, and $E(bY,b'Y) = b\sigma(b')\bigl(p(c) - c(w+\sigma(w))\bigr) + c\,b\bigl[\theta(b') - T(b') - \sigma(T(b'))\bigr] = 0$ by \eqref{eq:app-rank-8} and \eqref{eq:app-rank-8p}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Products of factors paired by the involution}

\begin{lemma}[Pierce preservation]\label{lem:app-pierce}
Let $A$ be reduced and let $e$ be a central idempotent with $\alpha(e) = e$. Then $D(Ae) \subseteq Ae$ and $D(A(1-e)) \subseteq A(1-e)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $x \in Ae$ and $y \in A(1-e)$. Then $x \star y = xy + \alpha(y)\alpha(x) = 0$, because $Ae \cdot A(1-e) = 0$ and $\alpha$ preserves each of the two pieces. Apply the projection $\pi_2$ onto $A(1-e)$, a homomorphism commuting with $\alpha$, to $H$ at $(x,y)$:
\[
0 = \pi_2(D(x \star y)) = \pi_2(D(x)) \star \pi_2(y) + \pi_2(x) \star \pi_2(D(y)) = q \star y ,
\]
where $q := \pi_2(D(x))$ and $\pi_2(x) = 0$. So $q \star y = 0$ for every $y \in A(1-e)$. With $y = 1-e$ this gives $q + \alpha(q) = 0$, so $\alpha(q) = -q$; with $y = q$ it gives $2q^2 = 0$, so $q^2 = 0$, and $q = 0$ because $A(1-e)$ is reduced. Hence $\pi_2(D(x)) = 0$ and $D(x) \in Ae$. Exchanging $e$ and $1-e$ gives the second inclusion.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:app-two-factor}
Let $A = B_1 \times B_2$, let $\theta \colon B_2 \to B_1$ be an isomorphism and put $\alpha(a_1,a_2) = (\theta(a_2), \theta^{-1}(a_1))$. Then $D$ is a derivation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The algebra isomorphism $\varphi(a_1,a_2) = (a_1, \theta(a_2))$ carries $\alpha$ to the swap, and both $H$ and $D\alpha = \alpha D$ are carried along by an algebra isomorphism, so we may assume $B_1 = B_2 = B$ and $\alpha(b,b') = (b',b)$.
Since $\alpha(b,b) = (b,b)$ and $\alpha(b,-b) = -(b,-b)$, there are additive $u, w \colon B \to B$ with
\[
D(b,b) = (u(b),u(b)), \qquad D(b,-b) = (w(b),-w(b)) .
\]
Here $x \star y = (x_1y_1 + y_2x_2,\ x_2y_2 + y_1x_1)$. Evaluating $H$ on the four kinds of pairs $(b,b)$ and $(b,-b)$ with $(c,c)$ and $(c,-c)$ gives
\begin{align}
u(bc+cb) &= u(b)c + cu(b) + bu(c) + u(c)b, \label{eq:app-tf-1}\\
u(bc+cb) &= w(b)c + cw(b) + bw(c) + w(c)b, \label{eq:app-tf-2}\\
w(bc-cb) &= u(b)c - cu(b) + bw(c) - w(c)b, \label{eq:app-tf-3}\\
w(bc-cb) &= w(b)c - cw(b) + bu(c) - u(c)b. \label{eq:app-tf-4}
\end{align}
\eqref{eq:app-tf-1} with $c = 1$ gives $bu(1) = -u(1)b$, and \eqref{eq:app-tf-4} with $c = 1$ gives $bu(1) = u(1)b$, so $2bu(1) = 0$ and $u(1) = 0$. \eqref{eq:app-tf-3} with $c = 1$ gives $bw(1) = w(1)b$, so $\mu := w(1)$ is central, and \eqref{eq:app-tf-2} with $c = 1$ gives $2u(b) = 2w(b) + 2\mu b$, that is $u = w + \mu\,\mathrm{id}$. Substituting this into \eqref{eq:app-tf-1} and \eqref{eq:app-tf-2} leaves the same bracket on the right and an extra $\pm\mu(bc+cb)$, so $2\mu(bc+cb) = 0$ for all $b, c$, and $b = c = 1$ gives $\mu = 0$. Thus $u = w$ and $u(1) = 0$, \eqref{eq:app-tf-1} and \eqref{eq:app-tf-2} coincide, as do \eqref{eq:app-tf-3} and \eqref{eq:app-tf-4}, and adding \eqref{eq:app-tf-1} to \eqref{eq:app-tf-3} gives $2u(bc) = 2u(b)c + 2bu(c)$. So $u$ is a derivation of $B$, and $D(b_1,b_2) = (u(b_1),u(b_2))$ is a derivation of $B \times B$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:app-orbits}
Let $A = \prod_{i \in I} B_i$ be reduced and suppose that $\alpha$ permutes the factors by an involution of $I$ without fixed points. Then $E \equiv 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For an orbit $O = \{i,j\}$ let $e_O = e_i + e_j$, where $e_i$ is the unit of the $i$-th factor. Then $e_O$ is a central idempotent with $\alpha(e_O) = e_O$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:app-pierce} $D$ maps $Ae_O$ into itself, and $D|_{Ae_O}$ is a derivation of $Ae_O$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:app-two-factor}, since $Ae_O \cong B_i \times B_j$ with $\alpha$ exchanging the two factors.
For $x, y \in A$ let $\pi_O \colon A \to Ae_O$ be the projection, a homomorphism commuting with $\alpha$ and, by the first part, with $D$. Then
\[
E(x,y)e_O = \pi_O E(x,y) = E(\pi_O x, \pi_O y) = E_{Ae_O}(\pi_O x, \pi_O y) = 0,
\]
and an element of $A$ vanishes if all its components $xe_O$ do. Hence $E \equiv 0$.
\end{proof}
